🪨Mississippi's Aquifers

Mississippi drinks ground water. Sixteen major aquifers lie stacked like layers of a cake under the state. Here's where each one is, how it works, how much water it gives, and what's happening to its water levels.

    Greg's take on Mississippi's aquifers

    Picture a stack of tilted layers, sand on clay on sand, with the oldest layers coming to the surface in northeast Mississippi and dipping down toward the southwest and the Gulf. Each sand is an aquifer. It fills where it reaches the surface and is under pressure where it dips under clay. Once you see that, well depths, artesian pressure, and why one town's water tastes different from the next all start to make sense.

    1 Why ground water matters here

    90%+of Mississippi's drinking water comes from ground waterMSU Extension
    16major aquifers, plus many minor onesMDEQ
    7,000 mi²of Delta alluvial plain over the state's most heavily pumped aquiferUSGS
    3rdThe Mississippi River Valley alluvial aquifer ranks third in the U.S. for total withdrawalsUSGS research

    Most Mississippi towns, rural water associations, schools, and farms pump their water from wells. The operators manual puts it at about 88 percent of the water used by public water systems, and USGS estimated about 87 percent of public-supply withdrawals in 2015. A few larger cities use surface water, including Jackson, which draws from the Ross Barnett Reservoir and the Pearl River.

    State law treats all of it as a shared resource. Mississippi Code §51-3-1 declares that all water, “whether occurring on the surface of the ground or underneath the surface of the ground,” belongs to the people of the state and is subject to regulation. MDEQ issues the withdrawal permits and studies the aquifers.

    How many aquifers? MDEQ describes 16 major aquifers and numerous minor ones. The operators manual counts 15 principal freshwater aquifers. The difference comes from how some units are grouped: several are really systems of related sands.

    2 The layer cake: how the aquifers are arranged

    Mississippi sits on the Mississippi Embayment, a huge trough that runs roughly along the Mississippi River and opens south toward the Gulf of Mexico. For about 100 million years, rivers and seas filled it with layers of sand, clay, chalk, and gravel. Those layers tilt, or dip, toward the axis of the embayment in the west and toward the Gulf in the south, and most of them get thicker as they go down-dip.

    • Oldest in the northeast. Paleozoic limestone and chert crop out in Tishomingo County. Next come the Cretaceous sands and chalk of northeast Mississippi.
    • Youngest in the south and the Delta. Miocene and younger sands and gravels lie near the coast, and river-laid sand and gravel underlie the Delta.
    • Outcrop = recharge area. Where a layer reaches the surface, rain soaks into it. That belt is the aquifer's recharge area, and the aquifer is unconfined there.
    • Down-dip = confined. Where the same sand dips under a clay layer, the water is under pressure. A well drilled into it is artesian: the water rises above the top of the aquifer, and sometimes flows at the surface.
    • Fresh water has a limit. Deep enough down-dip, water becomes too mineralized to drink. Dissolved solids above about 1,000 mg/L generally mark the edge of fresh water. On the coast, fresh water reaches about 1,200 feet deep east of Pascagoula and more than 3,000 feet in western Hancock County.

    Between the sands are confining units, clays and chalks that slow water down. The Yazoo Clay, the Cook Mountain Formation, the Zilpha Clay, the Porters Creek Clay of the Flatwoods, and the Selma chalk of the Black Prairie are the big ones. They're why a single drill hole can pass through several separate aquifers, each with its own water level and chemistry.

        3 Water levels: what the records show

        When wells pump more than an aquifer receives, water levels fall. In a confined aquifer that shows up as a drop in pressure, and the effect spreads for miles. These are declines documented in USGS studies of Mississippi aquifers. They come from different decades, so treat them as snapshots, not current rates.

        Aquifer and areaWhat was measuredRate

        The best-documented recovery is in Tupelo. Water levels in the Eutaw-McShan beneath Tupelo and West Point had fallen about 200 feet below early-1900s levels. After Tupelo began using water from the Tombigbee River, the water level beneath the city rose nearly 100 feet within about five years. Cutting pumping works, and a confined aquifer's pressure can come back relatively fast.

        !
        Exam watch · Reading water levels

        Static water level is measured with the pump off; pumping level is measured with it running. Drawdown = pumping level − static level. A long-term drop in the static level means the aquifer is declining; a drop in specific capacity (gpm per foot of drawdown) with a steady static level points to a clogging well.

        4 The Delta's alluvial aquifer: pressure and solutions

        The Mississippi River Valley alluvial aquifer (MRVA) underlies the whole Mississippi Alluvial Plain, which covers parts of seven states. In Mississippi that's the Delta: about 7,000 square miles in all or parts of 17 counties. It's sand and gravel laid down by the river, usually more than 75 feet thick, and capped by a layer of silt and clay.

        It is the state's most heavily pumped aquifer, and about 98 percent of what's pumped from it goes to agriculture: irrigating corn, soybeans, cotton, and rice, and filling catfish ponds. Across the whole alluvial plain, more than 12 billion gallons a day are withdrawn on average.

        Pumping has outpaced recharge in the central Delta, where a long-term cone of depression has formed. In the USGS spring 2024 water-level map, the lowest water-level altitude was about 60 feet above sea level, in Sunflower County. Wet years in the mid-to-late 2010s slowed declines, and levels even rose in some areas, but the long-term trend there has been down.

        What's being done

        • YMD, the Yazoo Mississippi Delta Joint Water Management District, was created in 1989 after the 1988 drought. It processes agricultural water-use permits (more than 25,000 of them), measures water levels, and promotes water-saving practices.
        • The Delta Sustainable Water Resources Task Force, started by MDEQ in 2011 and formalized by executive order in 2014, brings together YMD, farm groups, the Army Corps, USGS, NRCS, and MDEQ.
        • Farm practices: irrigation scheduling with soil moisture sensors, tailwater recovery ditches, on-farm storage reservoirs that catch runoff for later use (the first was installed in the Delta in 2010), land forming, and low-grade weirs.
        • Managed aquifer recharge: near Shellmound, in Leflore County, a pilot study pumps water from wells beside the Tallahatchie River (bank filtration) and uses it to recharge the alluvial aquifer where levels have dropped.
        • Mapping from the air: USGS flew airborne electromagnetic surveys over the Shellmound area in 2018 and over the whole alluvial plain in 2021–2022. The largest such survey ever flown in the U.S., it maps the sand, gravel, and clay underground so groundwater models are more accurate.

        The alluvial aquifer's water is usually hard to very hard and often high in iron and manganese, which limits its use for drinking water. Many Delta towns drill past it into deeper sands for their public supply.

        5 Mississippi v. Tennessee: who owns an aquifer?

        The Middle Claiborne aquifer, called the Sparta Sand in Mississippi and the Memphis Sand in Tennessee, lies under both states. Memphis's utility, Memphis Light, Gas and Water, pumps about 120 million gallons a day from it, from wells entirely inside Tennessee. Mississippi sued, arguing the pumping pulled Mississippi-owned water across the state line and was a taking of state property.

        On November 22, 2021, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously against Mississippi. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that the aquifer has a “multistate character.” Its water flows naturally between the states, and pumping in one state affects the aquifer in the other. So its water is subject to equitable apportionment, the same doctrine the Court uses to divide interstate rivers. A state doesn't own the part of an aquifer under its borders outright. Mississippi's complaint was dismissed without leave to amend. It was the first time the Court had applied equitable apportionment to ground water.

        6 Water quality in Mississippi's aquifers

        MDEQ's ground water assessments describe the overall quality of Mississippi's ground water as very good. The most notable natural issue is color: tea-colored water from dissolved organic matter in some aquifers. Common natural issues:

        IssueWhere it shows upTypical treatment
        Iron and manganeseThe alluvial aquifer, the Meridian-upper Wilcox, and many othersAeration or chemical oxidation, then filtration (such as greensand)
        HardnessThe alluvial aquifer (often hard to very hard), and the Coffee Sand and Ripley in the northeastIon exchange or lime softening where needed
        Color (tannins, humic material)Some aquifers and parts of aquifers, especially sands near lignite or other buried plant materialCoagulation and filtration, oxidation, or anion exchange; watch DBP formation
        CorrosivenessSoft, low-pH, or low-alkalinity water, such as some Meridian-upper Wilcox suppliespH and alkalinity adjustment, corrosion inhibitors
        Hydrogen sulfideScattered deeper wellsAeration, oxidation
        Salty (mineralized) waterDown-dip, beyond the fresh water limit; chloride in the Sparta increases down-dipStay in the fresh zone; watch chloride trends

        Ground water also warms with depth, about 1°F per 100 feet in the operators manual's rule of thumb, so deep wells deliver noticeably warm water.

        7 Who manages and protects ground water

        WhoWhat they do
        MDEQ Office of Land and Water ResourcesIssues ground water and surface water withdrawal permits (required for wells with a surface casing 6 inches or larger; a single household's domestic well is exempt), licenses water well contractors, maps aquifers, and tracks water levels.
        MSDH Bureau of Public Water SupplyApproves public supply wells, sets construction and disinfection requirements, and runs source water protection.
        YMDAgricultural water permits and water-level monitoring in the Delta.
        USGS Lower Mississippi-Gulf Water Science CenterLong-term water-level networks, aquifer studies, and groundwater models such as the Mississippi Embayment Regional Aquifer Study (MERAS) model.

        What operators can do: measure static and pumping levels on a schedule; track specific capacity; keep wellheads sealed and the ground around them clean; plug abandoned wells properly, since an open old well is a direct pipe into the aquifer; and support wellhead protection with local officials.
