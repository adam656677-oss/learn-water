Chapter 1 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

⚖️Safe Drinking Water

The federal and Mississippi law behind everything else you'll study — who regulates what, what a water system owes its customers, and how violations get reported.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    This chapter is pure rulebook, and it shows up on every exam no matter what class you're testing for. Learn the three system types cold, know the three big duties the law imposes on a water system, and understand the difference between an MCL and a treatment technique. That's 90% of the questions right there.

    1 The Safe Drinking Water Act

    Congress passed the original federal Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) in 1974, amending it in 1986 and 1996. It set up a partnership between the states and the EPA: a state can take on primacy — primary enforcement authority — as long as its own rules are no less strict than the federal ones. Every state except Wyoming has assumed primacy.

    Mississippi adopted the SDWA in 1975, with the rule that Mississippi's regulations would be no more strict than federal law. So in practice, Mississippi's drinking water rules are exactly as strict as the federal requirements — no looser, no tighter.

    The Mississippi State Department of Health (MSDH) is the primacy agency. Its Division of Water Supply (DWS) administers the program day to day. The owner or operator of the water system is the one responsible for meeting the SDWA's requirements — not MSDH.

    2 The three types of public water systems

    All publicly and privately owned public water supplies fall under the law. There are three major types, and telling them apart is one of the most common exam questions in the whole book:

    • Community water system — serves a year-round residential population. Needs at least 15 service connections or 25 residents. Examples: municipal, subdivision, mobile home park, and rural water systems.
    • Nontransient noncommunity system — serves at least 25 of the same people for at least six months a year. Examples: schools, factories, hospitals. These must meet the same requirements as community systems.
    • Transient noncommunity system — serves different people who don't live there. Examples: highway rest stops, restaurants, motels, golf courses, parks.

    3 Three requirements the law imposes

    The SDWA imposes three major duties on every water system:

    • Sampling and reporting — the system is directly responsible for monitoring microbiological and radiological contaminants, turbidity, and unregulated chemicals. In Mississippi, the Mississippi Public Health Laboratory does the actual analysis; MSDH supplies containers, transports samples, and reports results back.
    • Record keeping — copies of lab results (with who collected the sample), dates and locations of sampling points, records of violations and corrective steps, sanitary survey reports, and all other water-quality information. These records are public — a customer has the right to inspect them.
    • Public notification — the system must notify the public of any regulation violation.

    4 Violation tiers and public notification

    Violations are classed by seriousness. Tier 1 violations — the more serious kind — include failing to comply with a maximum contaminant level (MCL), a treatment technique, or a variance/exemption schedule. Tier 2 violations include failing to meet a monitoring requirement, a testing procedure, or a variance/exemption. An MCL violation means actual contamination and demands far more extensive public notification than, say, missing a sampling schedule.

    i
    Current rule check · Public notification

    The manual describes an older tier system. Under EPA's current Public Notification Rule (40 CFR 141, Subpart Q), Tier 1 covers acute risks, such as an E. coli or nitrate MCL violation, with notice within 24 hours. Tier 2 covers other MCL, MRDL, and treatment technique violations, with notice within 30 days. Tier 3 covers monitoring and reporting violations, with notice within one year (often in the Consumer Confidence Report). Since October 16, 2024, a lead action level exceedance also needs Tier 1 notice within 24 hours. Answer exam questions from the manual, and follow MSDH's instructions on the job.

    5 MCLs, MCLGs, and treatment techniques

    Primary standards protect public health and are enforceable — they're either a maximum contaminant level (MCL) or a treatment technique requirement. The MCL is the enforceable number a water sample must meet. An MCL goal (MCLG) is the level associated with zero health effects — for cancer-causing chemicals, that goal is set at zero. The MCLG is a goal, not an enforceable standard. Some contaminants are hard or costly to measure directly, so EPA instead requires a specific treatment technique — the Lead and Copper Rule and the Surface Water Treatment Rule are examples.

    Secondary standards cover aesthetic quality — taste, odor, color. They're not enforceable, but most customer complaints trace back to them anyway.

    i
    Current rule check · Lead and PFAS

    EPA's 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements lower the lead action level from 15 to 10 µg/L and require most lead service lines to be replaced within 10 years, with compliance starting November 1, 2027. EPA's first PFAS drinking water standards (2024) set enforceable limits of 4.0 ng/L for PFOA and PFOS. EPA said in 2025 it would keep those two limits, and in May 2026 it proposed letting systems request until 2031 to meet them and dropping the other PFAS limits. Check with MSDH for what applies to your system.

    6 Enforcement, variances, and emergency powers

    A system with genuine technical or financial trouble can seek a variance or exemption — but these are rare and hard to get, and the supplier has to prove there's no threat to public health. If a state fails to enforce the SDWA, the EPA can step in, and citizens can sue after giving 60 days' notice (class-action suits aren't allowed). In a true emergency posing an "imminent and substantial" danger, the EPA can issue orders directly; violating an EPA emergency order carries a fine of up to $25,000 per day, per violation.

    7 The federal rules behind your daily work

    The SDWA is carried out through a set of rules, each aimed at one kind of risk. You don't need to memorize the federal regulations, but you should know which rule drives each routine task on your system:

    RuleWhat it controlsWhat it means for operators
    Revised Total Coliform Rule (2016)Bacteria in the distribution systemRoutine samples from the siting plan, repeat samples after a positive, and assessments to find the cause (Chapter 6)
    Ground Water Rule (2006)Fecal contamination of wellsSanitary surveys, source sampling after a coliform-positive result, and fixing significant deficiencies (Chapter 5)
    Surface Water Treatment RulesGiardia, viruses, Cryptosporidium, and turbidityFiltration, disinfection, turbidity limits, and CT for surface water and GWUDI (Chapter 8 A)
    Lead and Copper RuleLead and copper from plumbingTap sampling at high-risk homes, corrosion control, a service line inventory, and public education (Chapters 7 and 9)
    Disinfectants and Disinfection Byproducts RulesTTHM, HAA5, chlorite, bromate, and disinfectant levelsKeeping byproducts under their MCLs and residuals under the MRDL (Chapter 8 A)
    Chemical and radionuclide rulesNitrate, arsenic, other inorganics, organics, and radionuclidesSampling each entry point on the schedule MSDH assigns

    A few MCLs come up again and again: nitrate 10 mg/L (as nitrogen), because it causes blue baby syndrome in infants; arsenic 0.010 mg/L; fluoride 4.0 mg/L; and the byproduct limits of 0.080 mg/L TTHM and 0.060 mg/L HAA5.

    8 Sanitary surveys

    A sanitary survey is MSDH's on-site review of your whole system, done to find weaknesses before they cause unsafe water. Federal rules require one at least every three years for community systems and every five years for noncommunity systems. (A state may stretch a community system with an outstanding record to five years.) Every survey covers eight areas:

    • Source: wells, intakes, and what surrounds them
    • Treatment
    • Distribution system
    • Finished water storage
    • Pumps, pump facilities, and controls
    • Monitoring, reporting, and data verification
    • System management and operation
    • Operator compliance with state requirements

    Problems that could let unsafe water reach customers are significant deficiencies. Under the Ground Water Rule, a system must consult with the state within 30 days of being notified, and correct the deficiency (or be on a state-approved schedule to correct it) within 120 days. The best preparation is ordinary good housekeeping: locked and screened wells and tanks, working meters and feeders, calibrated test equipment, and records that are complete and easy to find.

    9 Records and the Consumer Confidence Report

    Records prove your system did what the rules require, and they're public. Federal rules set these minimums, and MSDH can require more:

    RecordKeep at least
    Bacteriological and turbidity analyses5 years
    Chemical analyses10 years
    Actions taken to correct violations3 years after the last action
    Sanitary survey reports and related letters10 years
    Variance or exemption records5 years after it expires
    Public notices and Consumer Confidence Reports3 years

    Community systems also owe customers an annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR), a plain-language water quality report covering the previous calendar year. It's due to customers by July 1, and the system certifies to the state by October 1 that it was delivered. It lists the water source, each regulated contaminant that was detected and how it compares with the MCL, any violations, and who to call with questions. EPA's 2024 update to the rule, which takes effect in 2027, adds clearer wording and a second report each year for systems serving 10,000 or more people.

    !
    Exam watch · Manual answers vs. current rules

    If a question asks about Tier 1 and Tier 2 violations, answer from the manual's system: MCL and treatment technique violations are Tier 1; monitoring and testing violations are Tier 2. Only the MCL is enforceable; the MCLG is a goal. And the owner or operator of the system, not MSDH, is responsible for meeting the SDWA.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • 15 connections / 25 residents — minimum size to count as a community water system.
    • 6+ months/year, same people — nontransient noncommunity system.
    • 1974 / 1986 / 1996 — SDWA passed, then amended twice.
    • 1975 — Mississippi adopted the SDWA, matching federal strictness exactly.
    • $25,000/day/violation — maximum fine for violating an EPA emergency order.
    • 60 days notice required before a citizen lawsuit against a water system, state, or EPA.
    • 3 years / 5 years — sanitary survey frequency for community / noncommunity systems.
    • 30 / 120 days — consult on, then correct, a significant deficiency (Ground Water Rule).
    • 5 years for bacteriological records; 10 years for chemical records and sanitary surveys.
    • July 1 — Consumer Confidence Report due to customers; certify delivery to the state by October 1.
    • MCLs: nitrate 10 mg/L (as N), arsenic 0.010 mg/L, fluoride 4.0 mg/L.
