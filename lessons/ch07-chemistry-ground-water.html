🧪Chemistry of Ground Water

Ions, pH, alkalinity, hardness, and the chemistry behind coagulation — the foundation for every treatment chapter that follows.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    This is the chapter that makes the treatment chapters make sense. Get pH backwards (low number = acidic, more H⁺ ions) locked in, know the hardness ranges, and understand why alkalinity matters so much for coagulation. It's not hard chemistry — it's mostly vocabulary and a couple of key relationships.

    1 Ions and pH

    Dissolved materials separate into electrically charged particles called ions. pH measures hydrogen ion (H⁺) concentration on a scale of 0 to 14: as H⁺ increases, pH goes down. Pure water has equal H⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations at pH 7 — neutral. Below 7 is acidic, above 7 is alkaline/basic.

    Chlorine, alum, ferric sulfate, sulfuric acid, hydrofluosilicic acid, and carbon dioxide all lower pH. Lime, soda ash, sodium hydroxide (caustic soda), and hypochlorite all raise pH. Many Mississippi ground waters run low pH naturally because of dissolved carbon dioxide, which aeration can remove.

    2 Alkalinity

    Alkalinity measures water's ability to neutralize acid — increasing alkalinity raises pH and buffers it against swings. It comes mainly from hydroxide, carbonate, and bicarbonate ions, with bicarbonate providing most of it in natural water. Alkalinity is critical for coagulation: most ground water in Mississippi has enough natural alkalinity for coagulation to work, but some needs lime added first — always dosed separately from the coagulant, since lime and alum react with each other directly.

    3 Hardness

    Hardness is caused mainly by calcium and magnesium ions (also strontium, iron, manganese, aluminum). The Mississippi guideline scale:

    Classificationmg/l as CaCO₃
    Softunder 75
    Moderately soft/hard75-150
    Hard150-200
    Very hardover 200

    Carbonate (temporary) hardness can be removed just by heat, which drives off CO₂ and precipitates carbonate. Non-carbonate (permanent) hardness doesn't respond to heat and forms hard scale once its solubility limit is exceeded. Rule of thumb: where total alkalinity is equal to or greater than total hardness, all the hardness is carbonate; where alkalinity is less than hardness, the carbonate hardness equals the alkalinity and the rest is non-carbonate.

    4 Coagulation chemistry

    Coagulants react with alkalinity to form hydroxide floc (aluminum hydroxide, ferric hydroxide) that traps microorganisms, color, and turbidity as it settles. The standard tool for dialing in the right dose is the jar test: fill several jars with test water, add varying coagulant amounts, mix vigorously briefly then gently for a longer period to let floc form, then check settled water for color and turbidity to find the best dose, mixing time, and settling time.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • pH scale: 0-14, with 7 = neutral. Lower pH = more H⁺ ions = more acidic.
    • Hardness: soft <75, moderate 75-150, hard 150-200, very hard >200 mg/l as CaCO₃.
    • Bicarbonate provides most natural alkalinity in Mississippi ground water.
    • Never mix lime and coagulant in the same feed line — add lime first, separately.
