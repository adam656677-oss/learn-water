Chapter 9 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🚰Distribution & Storage

Designing, building, and maintaining the pipes, valves, and storage tanks that move treated water from the plant to the tap.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    The distribution system is the utility's biggest investment and its least visible — which is exactly why exam questions love it. Know the minimum pressures cold (20 psi minimum, 35-60 psi normal), the valve types, and the difference between pressure-tank storage and elevated storage. Both storage sections tie straight into hydraulics from Chapter 4.

    1 Design basics

    A good distribution design minimizes dead ends and maximizes looped lines so water keeps moving and stays fresh everywhere. The recommended minimum main size is 4 inches; a main supplying fire protection should be at least 6 inches. Domestic use requires a minimum of 20 psi during peak flow, and normal service pressure should run 35-60 psi. Common pipe materials are ductile iron, PVC, steel, and prestressed concrete — no single material is best for every situation; PVC resists corrosion well but is harder to locate underground and needs careful handling when tapping.

    2 Valves

    Gate valves handle routine shutoffs and are spaced so a main break never requires shutting down more than about 500 feet in a dense area (800 feet elsewhere). Check valves open with normal flow and close against reversed flow — an important defense against backflow contamination. Other special valves: air-relief valves release trapped air at high points; pressure-regulating valves knock down excess pressure; altitude valves shut off flow into a tank at a preset level to prevent overflow.

    3 Construction, disinfection, and maintenance

    New mains get a hydrostatic pressure test at at least 50% above normal operating pressure, held for at least an hour, checking for leaks. Before going into service, flush and disinfect new lines with at least 50 mg/l free chlorine held for 24 hours, ensuring at least 10 mg/l residual remains at the end of that period — and the post-disinfection sample must show no coliform bacteria. Ongoing maintenance: PRVs monthly, valves annually, hydrants twice a year, meters calibrated annually. Regular high-velocity flushing removes sediment, eliminates hydrogen sulfide odor, and keeps chlorine residual consistent throughout the system.

    4 Pressure (hydropneumatic) tanks

    Pressure tanks aren't real storage — they only smooth out pump cycling and provide brief chlorine contact time. As a rule of thumb, tank capacity should be at least 40 times the pump's capacity in gpm to give a reasonable cycle time. Only the water between the pump's "on" and "off" pressure settings is usable — a typical 15 psi range might only deliver 1,200-1,500 gallons before the pump kicks back on. Longer pump cycles mean less wear and lower electricity cost, since motor starting current can run 6-10 times normal run current.

    5 Elevated storage

    Elevated storage is the more reliable option: it lets a pump run at a steadier, more efficient head, provides real reserve capacity during power outages or well maintenance, and can supply fire flows that pressure-tank systems generally can't. RMDC recommends the water-level swing in an elevated tank or standpipe not exceed 30 feet. A standpipe's total volume isn't all usable storage — only the top portion above the minimum useful level counts (e.g., only the top 25 of 80 feet in a typical example, about 33,000 of 100,000 gallons).

    6 Flushing programs

    Flushing removes sediment, restores chlorine residual, and clears stale water from dead ends. There are two approaches:

    • Conventional flushing opens hydrants in an area until the water runs clear. It's simple, but it can stir up sediment without carrying it out.
    • Unidirectional flushing closes valves so water moves one way at high velocity, from clean mains toward dirty ones, working outward from the source. Aim for at least 2.5 ft/sec, and ideally about 5 ft/sec, to scour deposits.

    Before flushing, plan the sequence, notify customers and the fire department, and check that the valves work. While flushing, keep at least 20 psi everywhere in the system, open and close hydrants slowly to avoid water hammer, and watch turbidity and chlorine residual until the water is clear. Aim the discharge so it won't cause erosion or flooding, and dechlorinate water that could reach a stream.

    7 Main breaks and repairs

    1. Isolate the break by closing the nearest valves, keeping as much of the system in service and under pressure as possible.
    2. Protect the pipe. Keep the trench pumped out below the pipe so dirty water can't get in, and use a trench box or shoring when required (Chapter 11).
    3. Repair and disinfect. Swab or spray new pipe and fittings with a 1% chlorine solution, following AWWA Standard C651, then flush the repaired section.
    4. Restore service slowly, bleeding air out through hydrants or services.
    5. Sample as MSDH directs. If pressure dropped below 20 psi or contamination is suspected, issue a boil water notice under MSDH's direction.

    Record each break's location, pipe material, size, and cause. Over time the pattern shows which mains to replace first.

    8 Storage tanks and water age

    • Inspect often. Check that hatches are locked, vents and overflows are screened, the overflow has its air gap or flap valve, and there's no sign of vandalism. Many utilities have tanks professionally inspected and cleaned every 3 to 5 years.
    • Cycle the water. Letting the level rise and fall each day turns the water over. Water that sits too long loses its chlorine residual, warms up, and can grow bacteria or form more byproducts.
    • Protect the steel. Keep coatings in good shape and maintain the cathodic protection.
    • Disinfect after work. A tank taken out of service for cleaning or repair is disinfected (AWWA Standard C652) and sampled before it goes back online.

    Current rule check · Lead service lines

    Every community and nontransient noncommunity system had to submit an inventory of its service line materials by October 16, 2024, and keep it updated. Under EPA's 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, most systems must replace lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement service lines within 10 years, with compliance starting November 1, 2027. Lines of unknown material have to be identified along the way. Check with MSDH for your system's schedule.

    Exam watch · Pressures and sizes

    20 psi minimum at all times, 35–60 psi normal service, mains at least 4 inches (6 inches for fire hydrants), and new mains disinfected with 50 mg/L for 24 hours with at least 10 mg/L remaining. Pressure tanks smooth out pump cycling; elevated tanks are the real storage.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • Minimum main size: 4 inches (6 inches for fire protection mains).
    • Minimum system pressure: 20 psi at all times; normal service 35-60 psi.
    • New-main disinfection: 50 mg/l free chlorine, 24 hours, ending with ≥10 mg/l residual and clean coliform results.
    • Hydrostatic test: 1.5× normal operating pressure, held 1 hour.
    • Pressure tank sizing: capacity ≥ 40× pump capacity (gpm) for a reasonable cycle.
    • Elevated tank/standpipe water-level swing: max 30 feet (RMDC guideline).
    • Flushing velocity at least 2.5 ft/sec (5 ft/sec ideal), keeping 20 psi in the system.
    • Swab repair fittings with 1% chlorine solution (AWWA C651).
    • Service line inventory due October 16, 2024; lead line replacement under the LCRI starts November 1, 2027.
