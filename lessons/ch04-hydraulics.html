⚙️Hydraulics

How water at rest and in motion behaves — static and dynamic head, pressure, friction loss, flow measurement, and pumps.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    Hydraulics questions almost always reduce to one conversion: 1 foot of water = 0.433 psi, or flip it around and 1 psi = 2.31 feet of water. Learn that cold and you can answer most static-head questions in your head. Then know the difference between centrifugal and positive-displacement pumps — that distinction shows up constantly.

    1 Static head and pressure

    Static head (elevation head) is the level of water above a reference elevation, measured in feet — it represents potential energy. With no water moving, levels equalize to match the highest elevated tank or standpipe in the system. To find static head at a point, subtract that point's elevation from the system's highest water elevation.

    Water weighs 62.4 lbs per cubic foot. Work through the math on a one-square-foot column of water one foot tall, and you get the single most useful number in this whole chapter: 1 foot of water = 0.433 psi. Flip it around (1 ÷ 0.433) and 1 psi = 2.31 feet of water. This holds no matter the shape of the container — pressure depends only on height of water above the measurement point, never on the container's shape.

    2 Dynamic hydraulics and head loss

    Total head in a flowing system = elevation (static) head + velocity head + pressure head (the Bernoulli principle). Velocity head is usually small in distribution systems, since design velocities stay under 5 ft/sec to limit friction loss. As water moves through pipe, some head is lost to friction — from water molecules rubbing the pipe wall and each other, and from bends, valves, and fittings. This is friction head loss, and it's why pressure head in a moving system is always less than static head.

    Friction loss depends on flow rate, pipe diameter and length, and pipe roughness — captured by the Hazen-Williams C-factor. New PVC pipe has a C of about 150; new ductile iron about 130; old tuberculated cast iron can drop to 50 or lower. The rougher the pipe, the lower the C-factor and the higher the friction loss.

    3 Flow measurement

    Metering matters because unaccounted-for water — the gap between what's pumped and what's billed — signals leaks and unmetered usage. Common flow-measuring devices include the residential displacement meter, the venturi meter (which the chlorinator ejector also relies on), the orifice/weir plate, and magnetic flow meters that measure the voltage induced as water passes through a magnetic field. Rate-of-flow controllers use the venturi principle to hold a filter's flow rate steady.

    4 Pumps

    Water-system pumps fall into two families:

    • Positive displacement pumps (piston or rotary) deliver a constant volume regardless of downstream conditions. They're mainly used for feeding chemical solutions in small plants — simple, reliable, easy to maintain — but the pump will keep trying to force water even against a closed valve, which can rupture the discharge line or diaphragm if not protected.
    • Centrifugal pumps use a spinning impeller to fling water outward by centrifugal force, creating low pressure at the center that draws more water in. They must be primed (air in the casing stops them from pumping), give nonpulsating flow, need no internal valves, and have one point of peak efficiency on their head-discharge curve — the "characteristic curve" the operator should try to run near.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • 1 ft of water = 0.433 psi — the core static-pressure conversion.
    • 1 psi = 2.31 ft of water — the reverse conversion.
    • 62.4 lbs/ft³ — the weight (specific weight) of water.
    • Design distribution velocities stay under 5 ft/sec to minimize friction loss.
    • Hazen-Williams C-factor: new PVC ≈ 150, new ductile iron ≈ 130, old cast iron can fall below 50.
