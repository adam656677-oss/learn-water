Chapter 8 (Class D) · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🧴Water Treatment — Class D

Chlorination and fluoridation — the two treatment processes every Class D operator must master, since they're the only treatment most Class D systems apply.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    This is THE Class D treatment chapter — every operator, no matter the class, needs it cold. Get the chlorine curve straight (demand → combined residual → breakpoint → free residual) and memorize the ideal fluoride range. Both come up constantly.

    1 Disinfection and the chlorine curve

    Nearly every water system disinfects with chlorine because it works across a wide range of conditions and — unlike UV, ozone, bromine, or iodine — leaves a residual that keeps protecting the water after it leaves the plant. When chlorine is added, it reacts in stages, illustrated by the classic chlorination curve:

    1. Chlorine demand — chlorine first reacts with impurities (organic matter, nitrates, iron, manganese, hydrogen sulfide). This demand must be satisfied before any residual forms. Example: 1 mg/l of hydrogen sulfide consumes 2.1 mg/l of chlorine.
    2. Combined residual — once demand is met, remaining chlorine reacts with ammonia to form chloramines, which disinfect weakly and smell strongly of bleach.
    3. Breakpoint — adding still more chlorine destroys the chloramines, driving the combined residual down to its lowest point.
    4. Free residual — chlorine added past the breakpoint stays as free residual — the strongest, most reliable form of disinfection.

    Maintain a free residual throughout the distribution system: it confirms the demand has been met, keeps odor-causing chloramines low, and free residual disinfects better than combined residual. Contact time of 15 to 30 minutes is recommended, and disinfection is more effective at lower pH (hypochlorous acid, the stronger form, dominates at low pH) and higher temperature.

    2 Feeding chlorine

    Gaseous chlorine is nearly 100% available chlorine and highly corrosive — cylinders are stored away from pumps and electrical equipment, with vents near the floor since chlorine gas is heavier than air. High-test calcium hypochlorite (HTH) is a white or yellow-white granular solid, about 70% available chlorine. Liquid (sodium) hypochlorite is fed with hypochlorinators — usually positive-displacement pumps that can work against system pressures up to 100 psi.

    Feed-rate shorthand: ppm × gpm × 0.012 = pounds of chlorine per 24 hours. Example: a 500 gpm well needing a 2 ppm dose → 2 × 500 × 0.012 = 12 lbs/24 hrs.

    3 Fluoridation

    Fluoride reduces tooth decay — studies show a 50-65% drop in cavities among children who drink water with about 1 mg/l fluoride ion from infancy. The ideal concentration is 0.8 to 1.2 mg/l. Three chemicals are used to add it:

    • Sodium fluoride (NaF) — white, odorless salt, 90-98% pure, fed as a saturated (~4%) solution or dry powder.
    • Hydrofluosilicic acid (H₂SiF₆) — corrosive 22-30% liquid, fed undiluted or heavily diluted (20+ parts water to 1 part acid) since diluting it lightly causes precipitation.
    • Sodium silicofluoride (Na₂SiF₆) — white crystalline powder, usually fed dry in larger plants.

    All three are corrosive once mixed with water — use corrosion-resistant materials throughout, wear proper PPE, and never breathe the dust or vapors.

    i
    Current rule check · Fluoride

    The manual's 0.8–1.2 mg/l range reflects older guidance. Since 2015 the U.S. Public Health Service has recommended 0.7 mg/L for community water fluoridation. EPA's enforceable MCL is 4.0 mg/L and its secondary standard is 2.0 mg/L. EPA began an expedited review of fluoride science in 2025, and policy is changing in several places, so follow MSDH's current direction.

    4 Chlorine math for a well system

    Everything starts with dose = demand + residual. If your well water uses up 1.3 mg/L of chlorine and you want 0.7 mg/L of free residual leaving the well site, the dose is 2.0 mg/L.

    Pounds per day. A well produces 0.5 MGD. At a 2.0 mg/L dose: 2.0 × 0.5 × 8.34 = 8.3 lb/day of chlorine. As 65% HTH, that's 8.3 ÷ 0.65 = 12.8 lb/day.

    Bleach. 12.5% sodium hypochlorite holds about 1 pound of available chlorine per gallon, so the same 8.3 lb/day takes roughly 8 gallons of bleach a day. Buy only what you'll use in a few weeks, because bleach loses strength with time, heat, and sunlight.

    Reading residuals. The DPD free test shows free chlorine; the total test shows free plus combined. Combined = total − free. If total is 1.2 mg/L and free is 0.9 mg/L, then 0.3 mg/L is combined. A large combined share in a system that should run on free chlorine means you haven't reached the breakpoint.

    5 Running a hypochlorinator

    • Mix solution carefully. Dilute bleach with soft or treated water; hard water forms calcium scale that clogs lines and check valves. Mix in a clean, covered, labeled tank.
    • Check the feed every day. Compare the drop in the solution tank with the gallons pumped. A sudden change means a pump, valve, or line problem.
    • Keep the pump primed. Gas bubbles or a leaky suction fitting can air-lock the pump head, so the motor runs but nothing feeds. Most pumps have a bleed or priming valve.
    • Clean the injection point. Scale builds up at the injector and its check valve. Clean them on a schedule, following the manufacturer's directions.
    • Tie the feed to the well pump, so chlorine feeds only when water flows, and set an alarm for when the feed fails.
    • Store chemicals safely. Keep hypochlorite cool, dark, and away from acids; bleach mixed with acid releases chlorine gas. Keep HTH dry and away from oil, grease, and other organic material, which can start a fire.

    6 Fluoride and phosphate feed in practice

    A sodium fluoride saturator makes a solution with a steady strength of about 18,000 mg/L fluoride, which keeps the feed math simple:

    Gallons of saturator solution = (dose mg/L × gallons treated) ÷ 18,000

    To add 0.7 mg/L to 500,000 gallons: 0.7 × 500,000 ÷ 18,000 = 19.4 gallons. Subtract natural fluoride from the target first: if the raw water already has 0.2 mg/L and the target is 0.7 mg/L, feed only 0.5 mg/L.

    Test fluoride daily and compare the result with the amount fed. An overfeed is serious: stop the feed, find the cause, notify MSDH, and follow its instructions about flushing and customer notice. A fluoride feeder should never be able to run while the well pump is off.

    Polyphosphate sequesters (ties up) iron and manganese so they don't stain. It works at low levels, generally below about 1 mg/L of iron and manganese combined, and it must be fed before chlorine oxidizes the metals. Orthophosphate forms a film on pipe walls that reduces lead and copper corrosion. Feed either one at a steady dose and check the phosphate residual in the system.

    !
    Exam watch · The chlorine curve

    Know the order: chlorine demand first, then combined residual (chloramines) rising, then the breakpoint dip, then free residual rising again. Chlorine works better at lower pH because hypochlorous acid (HOCl) dominates there. HTH is about 65–70% available chlorine; chlorine gas is nearly 100%.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • Free chlorine residual: at least 0.2 mg/l through the distribution system; MRDL of 4.0 mg/l annual average.
    • Recommended chlorine contact time: 15-30 minutes.
    • HTH is about 70% available chlorine; gaseous chlorine is nearly 100%.
    • Feed-rate shorthand: ppm × gpm × 0.012 = lbs of chlorine per 24 hours.
    • Ideal fluoride concentration: 0.8-1.2 mg/l.
    • Combined chlorine = total − free; dose = demand + residual.
    • 12.5% bleach ≈ 1 lb of available chlorine per gallon.
    • Saturator solution ≈ 18,000 mg/L fluoride; gallons = dose × gallons treated ÷ 18,000.
