Chapter 8 (Class D) · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🧴 Water Treatment — Class D

Chlorination and fluoridation — the two treatment processes every Class D operator must master, since they're the only treatment most Class D systems apply.

🧑‍🏫

Greg's take on this chapter

This is THE Class D treatment chapter — every operator, no matter the class, needs it cold. Get the chlorine curve straight (demand → combined residual → breakpoint → free residual) and memorize the ideal fluoride range. Both come up constantly.

1 Disinfection and the chlorine curve

Nearly every water system disinfects with chlorine because it works across a wide range of conditions and — unlike UV, ozone, bromine, or iodine — leaves a residual that keeps protecting the water after it leaves the plant. When chlorine is added, it reacts in stages, illustrated by the classic chlorination curve:

  1. Chlorine demand — chlorine first reacts with impurities (organic matter, nitrates, iron, manganese, hydrogen sulfide). This demand must be satisfied before any residual forms. Example: 1 mg/l of hydrogen sulfide consumes 2.1 mg/l of chlorine.
  2. Combined residual — once demand is met, remaining chlorine reacts with ammonia to form chloramines, which disinfect weakly and smell strongly of bleach.
  3. Breakpoint — adding still more chlorine destroys the chloramines, driving the combined residual down to its lowest point.
  4. Free residual — chlorine added past the breakpoint stays as free residual — the strongest, most reliable form of disinfection.

Maintain a free residual throughout the distribution system: it confirms the demand has been met, keeps odor-causing chloramines low, and free residual disinfects better than combined residual. Contact time of 15 to 30 minutes is recommended, and disinfection is more effective at lower pH (hypochlorous acid, the stronger form, dominates at low pH) and higher temperature.

2 Feeding chlorine

Gaseous chlorine is nearly 100% available chlorine and highly corrosive — cylinders are stored away from pumps and electrical equipment, with vents near the floor since chlorine gas is heavier than air. High-test calcium hypochlorite (HTH) is a white or yellow-white granular solid, about 70% available chlorine. Liquid (sodium) hypochlorite is fed with hypochlorinators — usually positive-displacement pumps that can work against system pressures up to 100 psi.

Feed-rate shorthand: ppm × gpm × 0.012 = pounds of chlorine per 24 hours. Example: a 500 gpm well needing a 2 ppm dose → 2 × 500 × 0.012 = 12 lbs/24 hrs.

3 Fluoridation

Fluoride reduces tooth decay — studies show a 50-65% drop in cavities among children who drink water with about 1 mg/l fluoride ion from infancy. The ideal concentration is 0.8 to 1.2 mg/l. Three chemicals are used to add it:

  • Sodium fluoride (NaF) — white, odorless salt, 90-98% pure, fed as a saturated (~4%) solution or dry powder.
  • Hydrofluosilicic acid (H₂SiF₆) — corrosive 22-30% liquid, fed undiluted or heavily diluted (20+ parts water to 1 part acid) since diluting it lightly causes precipitation.
  • Sodium silicofluoride (Na₂SiF₆) — white crystalline powder, usually fed dry in larger plants.

All three are corrosive once mixed with water — use corrosion-resistant materials throughout, wear proper PPE, and never breathe the dust or vapors.

📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

  • Free chlorine residual: at least 0.2 mg/l through the distribution system; MRDL of 4.0 mg/l annual average.
  • Recommended chlorine contact time: 15-30 minutes.
  • HTH is about 70% available chlorine; gaseous chlorine is nearly 100%.
  • Feed-rate shorthand: ppm × gpm × 0.012 = lbs of chlorine per 24 hours.
  • Ideal fluoride concentration: 0.8-1.2 mg/l.
