🏭Water Treatment — Unit Processes

Aeration, coagulation/flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, ion exchange, iron/manganese control, and stabilization — the core Class B and C treatment train.

    Greg's take on this chapter

    This is the longest, densest chapter in the whole book — take it one unit process at a time. Notice how the processes chain together: aeration removes gases and oxidizes iron/manganese, coagulation/flocculation clumps particles, sedimentation settles the heavy floc out, and filtration polishes what's left. Learn the numbers for each stage and you'll be able to answer almost anything thrown at you.

    1 Aeration

    Aeration mixes air into water to remove troublesome gases (carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, methane), oxidize iron and manganese into a removable insoluble form, reduce certain tastes and odors, and add oxygen. Water containing more than 0.3 mg/l iron stains fixtures and clothing yellowish-brown; manganese above 0.05 mg/l can produce black stains. Aeration followed by 30 minutes of detention time oxidizes these metals, and filtration then removes the precipitate. Loading rates for gravity aerators run 10-30 gpm/ft². Hydrogen sulfide is dangerous in enclosed spaces — concentrations of just 0.1% gas-to-air can be fatal within 30 minutes — so adequate ventilation is essential.

    2 Coagulation and flocculation

    Most particles causing turbidity carry a negative charge and repel each other (the zeta potential). Coagulation is the rapid mixing of a positively-charged coagulant into the water to neutralize that charge — this reaction happens in one to two seconds, which is exactly why rapid mixing is so critical. Flocculation is the gentle mixing afterward that lets neutralized micro-floc collide and grow into larger, settleable floc.

    Coagulants are classified by charge: monovalent (1+), bivalent (2+), and trivalent (3+, like aluminum and iron) — trivalent coagulants are 700 to 1,000 times more effective than monovalent ones at neutralizing negative particles. Alum (aluminum sulfate) is the most common coagulant.

    3 Sedimentation

    Sedimentation settles out the heavier floc before filtration, ideally leaving effluent turbidity under 10 NTU. Every settling basin has four zones — inlet, settling, outlet, and sludge. Design criteria call for a minimum of four hours detention time (reduced to two hours for lime-soda softening treating only ground water), settling velocity under 0.5 ft/min, and a weir overflow rate not exceeding 20,000 gallons per day per foot of weir length. A typical overflow rate for alum floc is about 500 gpd/ft².

    4 Filtration

    Filtration is the last polishing step before water reaches customers. Most Mississippi plants use gravity, downflow rapid sand or dual-media (sand + anthracite) filters. Typical loading rates: about 2 gpm/ft² for single-media, 3 gpm/ft² for dual-media filters. Backwash when head loss reaches 7 to 10 feet, at rates of 15-20 gpm/ft² for sand filters or 10-15 gpm/ft² for dual media, typically for 3-20 minutes until the wash water runs clear.

    5 Ion exchange, iron/manganese control, and stabilization

    Ion exchange swaps one ion for another — usually sodium for calcium/magnesium (softening). It works best for low-hardness, low-TDS water (under 2,000 mg/l) and isn't suitable above 0.3 mg/l iron or manganese combined. Regeneration uses a strong salt (brine) solution over 20-35 minutes of contact time.

    Iron and manganese control methods include oxidation/detention/filtration, water softening, manganese greensand filtration, ion exchange, and polyphosphate sequestration. Potassium permanganate oxidizes manganese faster than chlorine and works across pH 5-9.

    Stabilization keeps finished water from being corrosive or scale-forming — the Langelier Saturation Index predicts which way a given water will go, valid within a pH range of 6.5-9.5. A slightly positive index deposits a thin, protective calcium carbonate coating on pipe walls; a negative index means corrosive water.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • Iron over 0.3 mg/l stains; manganese over 0.05 mg/l stains black.
    • Coagulation reaction time: 1-2 seconds — rapid mixing is critical.
    • Trivalent coagulants are 700-1,000× more effective than monovalent ones.
    • Sedimentation: minimum 4 hours detention (2 hrs for lime-soda softening); effluent turbidity target <10 NTU.
    • Filter backwash trigger: head loss of 7-10 feet.
    • Filtration rates: ~2 gpm/ft² single media, ~3 gpm/ft² dual media.
