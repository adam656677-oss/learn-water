Chapter 5 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🕳️Ground Water & Wells

Where Mississippi's water comes from, how aquifers work, and how a well is built, developed, and maintained.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    About 88% of Mississippi's public water systems run on ground water, so this chapter matters no matter what class you're studying for. Get confined vs. unconfined (water-table) aquifers straight, know your well-development vocabulary, and remember the disinfection numbers for a newly completed well — they show up on every class exam.

    1 Mississippi's ground water

    About 88 percent of the water used by Mississippi public water systems is ground water, and every public water system in the state uses at least some ground water as a primary or backup source. Mississippi has 15 principal freshwater aquifers. Water percolates down from rainfall, dissolving minerals along the way — mineral content increases with travel time until it reaches equilibrium, which is why chemistry can vary even within the same aquifer.

    2 Aquifer types

    The zone of saturation — where all the spaces between soil particles are filled with water — is the free ground-water reservoir, also called a water table aquifer or unconfined aquifer. A well completed here is a water table well; it's vulnerable to pollution from surface discharges and to water-table fluctuation.

    A confined aquifer has an impervious formation above and below it, so the water is under pressure — like water in a distribution main. If water rises above the top of the aquifer when a well taps it, that's an artesian aquifer; if it rises all the way to the top of the casing and overflows, that's a flowing artesian well.

    3 Measuring aquifers and wells

    • Transmissivity — the rate water flows through a vertical section of aquifer, expressed as ft²/day. Mississippi aquifers range from about 5 to over 84,000 ft²/day.
    • Permeability — how easily water moves through the aquifer material, in gallons per day per square foot.
    • Porosity — the percent of the formation's volume that's open space. High porosity doesn't guarantee high yield — clay can have plenty of pore space but yield almost nothing because the openings are too small.
    • Drawdown — the drop from static water level to pumping water level after stabilizing.
    • Specific capacity — gallons per minute the well produces per foot of drawdown. Divide pumping rate (gpm) by drawdown (ft) to calculate it.

    Mississippi ground water generally runs under 100 mg/l dissolved solids near an aquifer's outcrop area, and mineral content rises with depth — over 1,000 mg/l is usually considered the freshwater downdip limit. Temperature rises about 1°F for every 100 feet of depth.

    4 Well construction

    Most community supplies use drilled wells (2 to 48 inches in diameter), built with either the cable-tool percussion method (best for hard rock) or hydraulic rotary methods (best in unconsolidated soils). A casing lines the bore hole to stop it from caving and to keep the aquifer from being contaminated from the surface or other aquifers. Grouting fills the space between the casing and the bore-hole wall — usually a high-early-strength or neat cement — to seal out contamination and protect against corrosion.

    Well screens collect water while holding back formation material; screen selection comes from a sieve analysis of the aquifer's sand. Corrosion, not casing failure, is the more common cause of screen failure. Well development — over-pumping, surging, or jetting — removes fine sand, silt, and drilling mud around the screen so the well can produce at full capacity.

    5 Disinfecting a completed well

    After construction, pump the well until the water runs clear with no fine sand, then disinfect the well and adjacent aquifer using a 50 mg/l free chlorine solution held for 24 hours. Afterward, pump until two consecutive chlorine-free samples, collected at least two hours of continuous pumping apart, both show no coliform bacteria before the well goes into service.

    6 Measuring water levels and tracking well performance

    Many wells have an airline: a small tube of known length that runs down beside the pump, below the water level. Pump air into it until the gauge stops rising; that pressure equals the depth of water over the bottom of the tube. Then:

    Depth to water = airline length − (gauge psi × 2.31)

    Example. A 250-foot airline reads 60 psi. Water over the tube: 60 × 2.31 = 138.6 ft. Depth to water: 250 − 138.6 = 111.4 feet. Read it with the pump off for the static level, and after the pump has run a while for the pumping level. The difference is the drawdown.

    Check specific capacity (gpm ÷ drawdown) on a regular schedule and compare it with the original test. A steady decline means the screen, or the formation around it, is clogging. Many operators plan rehabilitation when specific capacity falls about 25% below the original, because plugging gets harder to remove the longer it's left.

    7 Protecting the wellhead

    Most well contamination gets in at the top, not through the aquifer. Good wellhead practice:

    • The casing extends above grade and above the floor of any pump house, and the ground slopes away so water drains off.
    • A sanitary seal or well cap keeps out insects, rodents, and surface water, and the casing vent is turned down and screened.
    • A concrete pad around the casing sheds rain and protects the grout seal.
    • No pits, and nothing stored nearby that could leak: fuel, pesticides, fertilizer, or chemicals.
    • Sewers, septic systems, and animal pens stay outside the protective distances MSDH requires, and the site is fenced and locked.
    • Abandoned wells are sealed with grout from the bottom up. An open or poorly plugged old well is a straight path from the surface into the aquifer.

    A community wellhead protection program goes further: it maps the land that recharges the well, inventories possible sources of contamination, and works with local officials to manage land use there.

    8 Troubleshooting and rehabilitating wells

    SymptomLikely causeWhat to do
    Falling yield and specific capacityScreen encrusted with iron, manganese, or calcium scale, or iron bacteriaRehabilitate: acid or chlorine treatment with surging or brushing, then redevelop
    Sand in the waterDamaged or corroded screen, wrong slot size, or pumping too hardLower the pumping rate, inspect with a downhole camera, and repair or replace the screen
    Red water, slime, or odorIron bacteria or sulfate-reducing bacteriaShock chlorinate, clean the well, and keep up routine disinfection
    Coliform in the raw waterSurface water entering at the wellhead, or a casing or grout failureInspect the wellhead, fix the defect, disinfect, and resample before use
    Pump runs but delivers little waterWater drawn down below the pump intake, or a worn pumpCheck water levels, pump amps, and discharge pressure

    After any work that opens the well, disinfect it and get satisfactory bacteriological results before putting it back in service.

    i
    Current rule check · Ground Water Rule

    When a routine distribution sample is total-coliform-positive, the Ground Water Rule requires a raw-water sample from every well in use at the time, collected within 24 hours. If a source sample is positive for a fecal indicator such as E. coli, the system must collect five more source samples within 24 hours, unless the state requires corrective action right away. A fecal-positive source sample requires Tier 1 public notice. Systems that provide and monitor 4-log treatment of viruses are exempt from this triggered source sampling.

    !
    Exam watch · Aquifer vocabulary

    A water table (unconfined) aquifer has no confining layer above it, so it's the most vulnerable to surface pollution. An artesian aquifer is confined; the well is a flowing artesian well only if water overflows the casing. Specific capacity is gpm ÷ drawdown, not the other way around.
