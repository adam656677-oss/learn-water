🕳️Ground Water & Wells

Where Mississippi's water comes from, how aquifers work, and how a well is built, developed, and maintained.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    About 88% of Mississippi's public water systems run on ground water, so this chapter matters no matter what class you're studying for. Get confined vs. unconfined (water-table) aquifers straight, know your well-development vocabulary, and remember the disinfection numbers for a newly completed well — they show up on every class exam.

    1 Mississippi's ground water

    About 88 percent of the water used by Mississippi public water systems is ground water, and every public water system in the state uses at least some ground water as a primary or backup source. Mississippi has 15 principal freshwater aquifers. Water percolates down from rainfall, dissolving minerals along the way — mineral content increases with travel time until it reaches equilibrium, which is why chemistry can vary even within the same aquifer.

    2 Aquifer types

    The zone of saturation — where all the spaces between soil particles are filled with water — is the free ground-water reservoir, also called a water table aquifer or unconfined aquifer. A well completed here is a water table well; it's vulnerable to pollution from surface discharges and to water-table fluctuation.

    A confined aquifer has an impervious formation above and below it, so the water is under pressure — like water in a distribution main. If water rises above the top of the aquifer when a well taps it, that's an artesian aquifer; if it rises all the way to the top of the casing and overflows, that's a flowing artesian well.

    3 Measuring aquifers and wells

    • Transmissivity — the rate water flows through a vertical section of aquifer, expressed as ft²/day. Mississippi aquifers range from about 5 to over 84,000 ft²/day.
    • Permeability — how easily water moves through the aquifer material, in gallons per day per square foot.
    • Porosity — the percent of the formation's volume that's open space. High porosity doesn't guarantee high yield — clay can have plenty of pore space but yield almost nothing because the openings are too small.
    • Drawdown — the drop from static water level to pumping water level after stabilizing.
    • Specific capacity — gallons per minute the well produces per foot of drawdown. Divide pumping rate (gpm) by drawdown (ft) to calculate it.

    Mississippi ground water generally runs under 100 mg/l dissolved solids near an aquifer's outcrop area, and mineral content rises with depth — over 1,000 mg/l is usually considered the freshwater downdip limit. Temperature rises about 1°F for every 100 feet of depth.

    4 Well construction

    Most community supplies use drilled wells (2 to 48 inches in diameter), built with either the cable-tool percussion method (best for hard rock) or hydraulic rotary methods (best in unconsolidated soils). A casing lines the bore hole to stop it from caving and to keep the aquifer from being contaminated from the surface or other aquifers. Grouting fills the space between the casing and the bore-hole wall — usually a high-early-strength or neat cement — to seal out contamination and protect against corrosion.

    Well screens collect water while holding back formation material; screen selection comes from a sieve analysis of the aquifer's sand. Corrosion, not casing failure, is the more common cause of screen failure. Well development — over-pumping, surging, or jetting — removes fine sand, silt, and drilling mud around the screen so the well can produce at full capacity.

    5 Disinfecting a completed well

    After construction, pump the well until the water runs clear with no fine sand, then disinfect the well and adjacent aquifer using a 50 mg/l free chlorine solution held for 24 hours. Afterward, pump until two consecutive chlorine-free samples, collected at least two hours of continuous pumping apart, both show no coliform bacteria before the well goes into service.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • 88% of Mississippi public water systems use ground water.
    • 15 principal freshwater aquifers in Mississippi.
    • Specific capacity = pumping rate (gpm) ÷ drawdown (ft).
    • New-well disinfection: 50 mg/l free chlorine held 24 hours, then two clean coliform samples.
    • Ground water temperature rises about 1°F per 100 ft of depth.
    • Over 1,000 mg/l dissolved solids is usually the freshwater downdip limit.
