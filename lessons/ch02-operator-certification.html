Chapter 2 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🎓Operator Certification

How Mississippi classifies water systems, and exactly what it takes for you to get — and keep — your certificate.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    This is the chapter that's literally about you. Memorize Table 2-1 — what makes a system Class A vs B vs C vs D — because it's the backbone of the whole licensing system. Then nail the renewal numbers: 48 hours vs 24 hours of continuing education, and the 30-day grace window.

    1 Who needs a certified operator

    Every community and nontransient noncommunity public water supply in Mississippi must have a certified waterworks operator — defined as "the person who directly supervises and is personally responsible for the daily operation and maintenance" of the system. MSDH has legal authority to certify operators; the Certification, Training and Monitoring Branch of the Division of Water Supply administers it. A waterworks operator's certificate must at least equal the system's classification, and any system that loses its certified operator must replace that person within 180 days.

    2 How systems are classified (Table 2-1)

    Every public water system is classified by the type of treatment it uses, from simplest to most complex:

    • Class D — one or more wells, no treatment beyond chlorination, fluoridation, and phosphate addition.
    • Class C — aeration, pH adjustment, corrosion control, or closed-pressure treatment including zeolite softening or iron removal.
    • Class B — two or more treatment facilities of different types, or iron/manganese removal facilities that break pressure or need flocculation and/or sedimentation.
    • Class A — surface water treatment, lime softening, or coagulation and filtration to remove constituents other than iron or manganese.
    • Class E — systems that purchase water only, or operators whose only job is running a distribution system.

    Notice the classes build on each other: Class A is the most demanding, Class D is entry-level. That's why every higher class's certification exam still covers the D-level material underneath it.

    3 Getting certified

    Every class requires the applicant to have graduated an accredited high school (or GED) at minimum, with Class A also accepting a bachelor's degree in engineering/applied science plus one year of Class A plant experience in place of the experience-ladder path. Every candidate needs at least one year of supervised experience, under a supervisor holding a certificate equal to or higher than the one being sought — and that supervisor must endorse the application. Two additional MSDH-certified operators must also recommend the applicant. The written exam is offered three times a year, after a four-day waterworks operators short course, and requires an exam fee.

    4 Renewal and continuing education

    Certificates are valid for three years. To renew without re-testing:

    • File the renewal application within 30 days after the existing certificate expires.
    • If continuously licensed less than 9 years: show proof of 48 approved continuing education units within the three-year certificate period.
    • If continuously licensed more than 9 years: only 24 units are required.

    Miss the 30-day window, or fail to get the required continuing education hours, and you must pass the written exam again to renew. Operators are responsible for keeping their own education records.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • 180 days — deadline to replace a lost certified operator.
    • 1 year minimum supervised experience required for any certification.
    • 3 years — how long a certificate stays valid.
    • 48 CEUs if licensed under 9 years; 24 CEUs if licensed 9+ years, to renew.
    • 30 days — grace window to file for renewal after expiration without re-testing.
    • 2 additional certified operators must recommend a new applicant, plus 1 supervising operator's endorsement.
