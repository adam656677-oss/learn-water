Chapter 6 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🦠Microbiology

The organisms that make water unsafe, how coliform testing works, and exactly how bacteriological samples get collected and analyzed.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    Coliform is the star of this chapter — know why it's the indicator organism, how many sample sites you need, and the exact bottle-handling procedure. Sample collection technique questions are everywhere on this material because a botched sample gives a false answer, and that's a real public-health risk.

    1 Why coliform is the indicator organism

    Coliform and E. coli are normal residents of the intestines of humans and animals, and they're present in sewage. Testing for every possible pathogen individually is impractical — they're hard to isolate and testing takes too long. Coliform makes an ideal stand-in because it's: always present when sewage is present, always absent when it isn't, survives longer in water than pathogenic species, and is easy and cheap to isolate and identify. A coliform-positive sample doesn't mean the water is dangerous by itself — it means sewage contamination is possible and needs investigating.

    2 Types of microorganisms

    Bacteria reproduce by binary fission, potentially dividing every 20-30 minutes under good conditions. They're classed by oxygen need (aerobic, anaerobic, facultative anaerobic) and by shape (coccus, bacillus, spirillum — over 80% of bacteria are bacillus/rod-shaped). Viruses are far smaller than bacteria and much harder to kill with chlorine — bacteria die in about 10 minutes at 0.2-0.3 ppm free residual, but enteric viruses need about 30 minutes of contact time at the same concentration.

    Protozoa like Giardia lamblia and Cryptosporidium form resistant cysts/oocysts that survive for months and resist disinfectants far better than bacteria or viruses. Cryptosporidium oocysts are small enough to pass even well-maintained filters, so keeping them out of the finished water requires multiple barriers — coagulation, filtration, and disinfection together, not any one alone.

    3 Sampling frequency and site selection

    EPA regulations recognize four sample types: initial, routine, check (re-sample), and special purpose. Regulations require a minimum of five sample sites per system, chosen from a state-approved sample siting plan, representative of the whole distribution system — including different source areas, storage facilities, and high/low pressure zones. Never sample from a leaking or dirty faucet, one within about 12 inches of the ground, or a fire hydrant.

    Minimum monthly sample counts scale with population — for example, systems serving 25 to 1,000 people need only 1 sample per month, while systems over a few million need well over 400. A check sample confirms or disproves a routine sample that exceeded an MCL, and check sampling continues until results show the contamination is gone.

    i
    Current rule check · Revised Total Coliform Rule

    Since April 1, 2016, the Revised Total Coliform Rule governs coliform monitoring. A total-coliform-positive routine sample must be followed by at least three repeat samples within 24 hours (the manual's “check samples”). Every positive is tested for E. coli, and the old total-coliform MCL was replaced by an E. coli MCL plus Level 1 and Level 2 assessments to find and fix the cause.

    4 Collecting a bacteriological sample

    Use only the sterile 100-ml bottles MSDH provides — they already contain sodium thiosulfate to neutralize any chlorine. Never add chlorine, bleach, or other disinfectant, and never heat the sample. The procedure:

    1. Run the faucet 2-3 minutes to flush the plumbing.
    2. Check water temperature and chlorine residual.
    3. Turn the faucet off, then sterilize it with a torch or alcohol-soaked cotton (careful not to burn the rubber washer).
    4. Turn the faucet back on to a moderate, steady stream, then fill the bottle just above the 100-ml mark without touching the rim or cap interior.
    5. Complete the sample card fully and secure it to the bottle.

    The sample must reach the lab for analysis within 30 hours of collection, or it's rejected and must be re-collected. The MSDH lab uses the Colilert method; any total-coliform-positive sample is automatically checked for E. coli.

    5 After a positive result

    Under the Revised Total Coliform Rule, a total-coliform-positive routine sample starts a clock:

    1. Repeat samples within 24 hours: at least three, one at the original tap and one each within five service connections upstream and downstream (MSDH may approve other sites).
    2. E. coli testing: every total-coliform-positive sample is tested for E. coli.
    3. Extra routine samples next month: a system that normally samples quarterly or annually must collect at least three routine samples the following month.

    An E. coli MCL violation occurs when an E. coli-positive repeat follows a total-coliform-positive routine sample, when a total-coliform-positive repeat follows an E. coli-positive routine sample, when a system doesn't collect all its repeats after an E. coli-positive routine sample, or when it doesn't test a total-coliform-positive repeat for E. coli. It requires Tier 1 notice within 24 hours.

    Other results trigger an assessment to find and fix the cause. A Level 1 assessment, done by the system, is triggered when a system taking fewer than 40 samples a month has two or more total-coliform-positive samples in a month (more than 5% for larger systems), or misses repeat samples after a positive. A Level 2 assessment, done by the state or an approved party, follows an E. coli MCL violation or a second Level 1 trigger within 12 months. Either one is due within 30 days of learning of the trigger.

    6 Waterborne disease: why the rules exist

    TypeExamplesWhat to know
    BacteriaSalmonella (typhoid), Shigella, Vibrio cholerae, E. coli O157:H7, CampylobacterKilled readily by free chlorine
    VirusesHepatitis A, norovirus, rotavirusNeed more contact time than bacteria
    ProtozoaGiardia lamblia, CryptosporidiumCysts resist chlorine, so filtration is essential for surface water

    Two outbreaks show what's at stake. In Milwaukee in 1993, Cryptosporidium passed through a treatment plant during a stretch of high turbidity and sickened an estimated 400,000 people. In Walkerton, Ontario, in 2000, E. coli O157:H7 from farm runoff reached a shallow well while chlorination was inadequate, killing seven people and sickening about 2,300. Walkerton's operators had also falsified their records. Both disasters led to stricter rules.

    The lesson is the multiple-barrier approach: protect the source, treat properly, disinfect with enough contact time, keep a residual and positive pressure in the distribution system, and monitor so problems are caught early.

    7 Boil water notices and emergency disinfection

    A boil water notice tells customers to boil water before drinking or cooking with it when contamination is possible: after pressure falls below 20 psi during a main break, after a positive E. coli result, or when treatment fails. Follow MSDH's direction on when to issue a notice, which areas it covers, and when it can be lifted, which requires satisfactory bacteriological samples.

    The standard advice to the public: bring water to a rolling boil for one minute, then let it cool. If boiling isn't possible, add 8 drops (about 1/8 teaspoon) of unscented 6% household bleach per gallon (6 drops of 8.25% bleach), stir, and let it stand 30 minutes. The water should have a slight chlorine smell.

    Afterward, flush the affected mains, confirm chlorine residuals, and collect the bacteriological samples MSDH requires.

    !
    Exam watch · Sample handling

    Bacteriological samples go in the sterile 100-ml bottle with sodium thiosulfate, reach the lab within 30 hours, and never come from a fire hydrant, a leaking faucet, or a tap within about 12 inches of the ground. A coliform-positive result is a warning sign, not proof of pathogens; an E. coli-positive result points to fecal contamination.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • 5 sample sites — the regulatory minimum per system, chosen from an approved siting plan.
    • 30 hours — maximum time from sample collection to lab analysis.
    • 0.2-0.3 ppm free chlorine, 10 minutes kills bacteria — but enteric viruses need about 30 minutes at the same level.
    • 100-ml sterile bottles, containing sodium thiosulfate, are required for every bacteriological sample.
    • Coliform testing traces back to sewage contamination — it is not itself the pathogen of concern.
    • 3 repeat samples within 24 hours after a coliform-positive routine sample.
    • Level 1 or Level 2 assessment due within 30 days of the trigger.
    • Boil water: rolling boil for 1 minute; emergency bleach: 8 drops per gallon (6%), wait 30 minutes.
    • Milwaukee, 1993: about 400,000 sickened by Cryptosporidium.
