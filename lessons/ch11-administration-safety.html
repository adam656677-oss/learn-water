Chapter 11 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🦺Administration & Safety

Running a water utility — organization, customer service, purchasing, maintenance, and the safety program that protects everyone on staff.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    This chapter reads more like a management textbook than an engineering one, but don't skim it — the 88% statistic about accident causes and the utility's service obligations both show up as exam questions. Focus on who's responsible for what: the PSC regulates service areas, the utility owns safety, and every employee owns their own conduct.

    1 Utility organization and service obligations

    Even a one-person water system performs all the same functions a large utility does: supply, treatment, distribution, metering, billing, legal, accounting, engineering, personnel, and public relations. A water utility is required to serve everyone within its service area, give equal and adequate service, and charge the same rate for the same service. In Mississippi, service areas for private systems and water associations are regulated by the Public Service Commission (PSC); cities can serve up to one mile outside city limits if no other entity already has exclusive rights there.

    A utility can discontinue service for nonpayment, unsafe customer plumbing, fraud, or noncompliance — but a cutoff policy has to be enforced fairly and swiftly, and disputed bills need a written, evenly-applied policy, not ad hoc decisions.

    2 Purchasing, stores, and maintenance

    Centralizing purchasing avoids duplicate stock and gets better prices; a purchase order should always be checked against the vendor's invoice and the receiving report before payment. Physical inventory counts should happen at least once a year, performed by someone outside the group responsible for day-to-day stores control.

    Equipment maintenance splits into two types: breakdown maintenance (fixing something that already failed, needing immediate action) and preventive maintenance (scheduled "fix it before it breaks" work). A water utility can't run on breakdown maintenance alone — 100% standby capacity for every piece of equipment isn't economically realistic, so a solid preventive program is what actually keeps unplanned failures rare.

    3 Safety program and accident prevention

    According to industry studies, roughly 88 percent of accidents are caused by specific unsafe acts by employees — which is exactly why training and safe-work procedures matter more than almost anything else in a safety program. A well-designed program needs clear leadership, training, safe working conditions, an accident-reporting system, first-aid readiness, and personal responsibility from every employee. Accident reports exist to find root causes and prevent repeats — not to assign blame — and should be filed as soon as possible after the incident so details aren't lost.

    MSDH recommends every utility have at least one person professionally trained in first aid, with first-aid kits stocked on utility trucks and at fixed locations. Indirect accident costs (lost time, damaged equipment, supervisor time) can run about four times the direct medical/compensation costs — which is why prevention is the cheaper path every time.

    4 Public relations

    Every customer touchpoint — the bill, the meter reader, a phone call — shapes how the public sees the utility. Good practice: route every complaint consistently, never bounce a customer between staff, stay patient with an upset caller, and give the most complete, factual information available. For media contact, know your material in advance, never say anything "off the record," and keep statements to radio/TV brief since only a few seconds typically air.

    5 Confined spaces

    Tanks, vaults, wet wells, valve pits, and manholes are confined spaces: big enough to enter, with limited ways in and out, and not meant for people to stay in. Many are permit-required because they can hold a dangerous atmosphere. Before anyone goes in:

    1. Test the air from outside, top to bottom, in this order: oxygen (safe range 19.5–23.5%), flammable gases (below 10% of the LEL), then toxic gases such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon monoxide.
    2. Ventilate with a blower, and keep testing while people are inside.
    3. Lock out pumps, valves, and lines that could flood the space or start machinery.
    4. Post an attendant outside who stays in contact and never goes in.
    5. Have a rescue plan and retrieval equipment ready.

    More than half of confined space deaths are would-be rescuers who went in without protection. If someone collapses, call for help and use the retrieval line; don't go in after them unless you're trained and equipped.

    6 Trenches and work zones

    • Trenches 5 feet or deeper need a protective system (sloping, shoring, or a trench box) unless they're dug in stable rock. Trenches 20 feet or deeper need a system designed by a registered professional engineer.
    • A competent person inspects the trench daily and after rain or anything else that changes conditions.
    • Keep spoil piles and equipment at least 2 feet back from the edge.
    • Provide a ladder or other way out within 25 feet of every worker in trenches 4 feet or deeper.
    • Call 811 before digging, and wait until buried utilities are marked.
    • In the street, set up signs and cones following the traffic control manual (MUTCD), and wear high-visibility clothing.

    7 Lockout/tagout and emergency planning

    Lockout/tagout keeps equipment from starting while someone works on it:

    1. Tell the affected workers you're shutting the equipment down.
    2. Shut it down with the normal controls.
    3. Isolate every energy source: electrical disconnects, valves, and stored pressure.
    4. Put your own lock and tag on each isolation point. Only the person who applied a lock removes it.
    5. Release stored energy: bleed off pressure and block parts that could move.
    6. Verify by trying to start the equipment, then return the controls to off.

    Plan for bigger emergencies, too. Under America's Water Infrastructure Act of 2018, community systems serving more than 3,300 people must complete a Risk and Resilience Assessment and an Emergency Response Plan, certify them to EPA, and review them every five years. Every system, large or small, benefits from a written plan with emergency contacts, backup power and water sources, and mutual aid agreements such as MsWARN.

    Exam watch · Safety numbers

    Oxygen 19.5–23.5%, flammables under 10% of the LEL, trench protection at 5 feet, spoil 2 feet back, and a way out within 25 feet. From the manual: about 88% of accidents come from unsafe acts, and indirect costs run about four times the direct costs.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • ~88% of workplace accidents trace back to a specific unsafe act by an employee.
    • Indirect accident costs run about 4× the direct costs (medical, compensation).
    • Physical stores inventory: at least once a year, by someone outside day-to-day stores control.
    • The Public Service Commission regulates private water utility service areas in Mississippi.
    • Cities may serve up to 1 mile outside city limits absent an existing exclusive provider.
    • Confined space entry: oxygen 19.5–23.5%, flammables below 10% of the LEL.
    • Trenches: protection at 5 ft, spoil 2 ft back, exit within 25 ft (4 ft or deeper).
    • America's Water Infrastructure Act: risk assessment and emergency plan for systems over 3,300 people, reviewed every 5 years.
