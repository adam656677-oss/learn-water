Chapter 12 · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🚱 Cross-Connection Control

How contaminated water can flow backward into the drinking-water system — and the five devices that stop it.

🧑‍🏫

Greg's take on this chapter

Match the device to the hazard, every time — that's the whole game in this chapter. Air gap for the worst hazards, RPZ when an air gap isn't practical, double-check for low-to-intermediate non-health hazards, and vacuum breakers only where there's no back-pressure risk. Get that hierarchy locked in.

1 Cross connections and backflow

A cross connection is any piping arrangement connecting a potable line to potentially contaminated water — either pipe-to-pipe, or a potable outlet submerged in contaminated water. Most happen unintentionally; a gate or check valve alone doesn't count as protection since they can leak undetected. Backflow is what happens when that connection reverses flow direction, letting contamination into the potable system. There are two causes:

  • Back pressure — the contaminated side's pressure exceeds the potable line's pressure (e.g., non-potable water pumped or stored at a higher elevation).
  • Back-siphonage — a vacuum forms in the water line (from a main break or nearby firefighting) and draws contaminated water in.

Hazard is classed high (could cause death, illness, or spread disease) or low (a nuisance, not a health hazard) — and the toxicity of the potential contaminant is what actually drives device selection.

2 The five backflow-prevention devices

DeviceProtects againstBest for
Air gap (AG)BothAny toxic hazard; physical separation, ≥ 2× pipe diameter, never less than 1 inch
Atmospheric vacuum breaker (AVB)Back-siphonage onlyNon-continuous-pressure uses (hose bibs, sprinklers) — must sit 12" above highest use point
Pressure vacuum breaker (PVB)Back-siphonage onlySame uses as AVB, but works under continuous pressure
Double check valve assembly (DC)BothLow-to-intermediate (polluted, not contaminated) hazards; head loss usually < 10 psi
Reduced pressure zone (RPZ)BothHigh-hazard installations where an air gap isn't practical; head loss 10-20 psi

All backflow-prevention devices need annual testing. An RPZ works via two spring-loaded check valves with a relief valve between them — if the second check valve fails, the relief valve vents to atmosphere rather than letting contamination back into the system, and a bypass line around an RPZ must also contain its own RPZ.

3 Whose job is it?

Everyone shares responsibility:

  • The customer — owns everything from the meter to the sewer, and must install/test/maintain backflow prevention at their own expense if a cross connection exists.
  • The water supplier — responsible from the source through the entire distribution system up to the meter: surveying regularly, educating the public, eliminating cross connections, and maintaining at least 20 psi to reduce back-siphonage risk.
  • The plumber — the first line of defense, catching potential cross connections during installation and reporting them immediately.
  • MSDH — enforces the rules and ensures water suppliers run adequate internal-protection and backflow-prevention programs.

📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

  • Air gap: at least 2× the supply pipe diameter, never less than 1 inch.
  • All backflow devices require annual testing.
  • AVB/PVB must sit at least 12 inches above the highest point of use.
  • Double-check assembly head loss: usually under 10 psi; RPZ head loss: 10-20 psi.
  • Maintain at least 20 psi distribution pressure to reduce back-siphonage risk.
