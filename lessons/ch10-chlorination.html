🛢️Chlorination Equipment & Safety

Cylinders, chlorinators, hypochlorinators, and the safety procedures that keep chlorine gas from becoming a hazard.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    Chlorine gas is genuinely dangerous, so this chapter is as much safety training as it is equipment knowledge. Learn the cylinder sizes, the ammonia leak-test trick, and why vents always sit near the floor. These aren't trivia — they're the procedures that keep you alive around chlorine gas.

    1 Chlorine supply and dosing

    A properly treated plant should already be free of microbiological contamination — chlorine's job is guarding against small leaks and back-siphonage in the distribution system. Recommended residual near the end of the lines is about 0.5 mg/l free chlorine, though some systems find that hard to hold and others exceed it comfortably. Batch chlorination (using sodium or calcium hypochlorite) handles one-off jobs like disinfecting a contaminated tank; automatic chlorination feeds continuously, sized to the volume pumped, chlorine demand, and desired residual.

    2 Cylinders and containers

    Gaseous chlorine ships in 150-pound cylinders (good for plants under 0.5 mgd, roughly 92-pound tare weight) or 2,000-pound (one-ton) containers (about 1,300 lbs empty, ~3,300 lbs full). Both have fusible plugs that melt at 158-165°F to relieve pressure before the cylinder can rupture in a fire. Never use wrenches longer than 6 inches, or pipe wrenches, on chlorine valves — open a stuck valve by striking the wrench heel, never by forcing it with extension bars. Store and transport cylinders upright, secured, away from direct heat and sunlight, and never near turpentine, ether, ammonia, or other flammable/reactive materials.

    3 Finding leaks and staying safe

    Chlorine gas is 2.5 times heavier than air, so it settles to the floor — that's why room vents and discharge fans are placed low, and why a chlorine cylinder should never be stored below ground level. To find a leak, hold an ammonia-soaked cloth or bottle near the suspected spot: ammonia and chlorine gas combine into a visible white cloud. Never pour ammonia directly onto a leak. Always wear an approved gas mask when entering a room with a possible chlorine leak, and never handle a leak alone — have a co-worker stand by.

    4 How a gas chlorinator works

    Most gaseous chlorinators use a vacuum system for safety: a booster pump forces water through an ejector, creating a vacuum that opens the ejector check valve, which in turn opens the chlorinator's inlet valve to draw gas from the cylinder. A rotometer shows the feed rate in pounds per 24 hours. If the booster pump stops, the vacuum collapses and the chlorinator's safety valves close automatically, stopping gas flow — a critical fail-safe. Size the chlorinator so normal operation sits at only one-fourth to one-third of the rotometer's scale, leaving headroom for higher demand.

    5 Changing a cylinder safely

    1. Have your SCBA ready and a coworker present, and check that the ventilation fan works.
    2. Close the empty cylinder's valve and let the chlorinator draw the remaining gas out of the line.
    3. Disconnect the yoke and put the protective cap back on the empty cylinder's valve outlet. Mark the cylinder empty.
    4. Chain the full cylinder in place, remove its valve cap, and inspect the valve.
    5. Connect with a new lead gasket every time. Never reuse one.
    6. Open the valve slowly, no more than one full turn, and leave the wrench on it so it can be closed fast.
    7. Check every connection for leaks with ammonia vapor before you walk away, and record the date and cylinder weights.

    6 What chlorine does to the body

    Chlorine in airEffect
    about 0.3 ppmMost people can smell it
    1–3 ppmMild irritation of the eyes, nose, and throat
    10 ppmImmediately dangerous to life and health (IDLH)
    30 ppmChest pain, coughing, vomiting, and trouble breathing
    about 1,000 ppmCan kill within minutes

    OSHA's exposure limit is a ceiling of 1 ppm. Chlorine forms acids with the moisture in the eyes, throat, and lungs, and fluid can build up in the lungs hours after an exposure.

    First aid: get the person to fresh air, but only if you can do it without exposing yourself; keep them calm and at rest; and call 911. Give oxygen only if you're trained. Flush eyes and skin with water for at least 15 minutes. Anyone with a real exposure needs a medical evaluation, even if they feel better.

    7 Chlorine rooms and emergencies

    • The room: separate from the rest of the building, with a door that opens outward to the outside and a window so you can look in before entering. The exhaust fan pulls from near the floor, and its switch is outside the door.
    • Leak detection: a chlorine gas detector with an alarm, tested regularly.
    • Protective gear: SCBA stored outside the chlorine room, where you can reach it during a leak.
    • Repair kits: the Chlorine Institute's Kit A fits 100- and 150-lb cylinders and Kit B fits ton containers. Train with them before you need them.
    • Never spray water on a leaking container. Water and chlorine make acid that makes the leak worse. If you can, turn a leaking container so gas escapes instead of liquid, which releases far more chlorine.
    • Plan ahead: keep an emergency plan, post emergency numbers, and coordinate with the local fire department. A facility that keeps more than 2,500 pounds of chlorine in a process must also meet EPA's Risk Management Program requirements.

    Exam watch · Leak and valve rules

    Find leaks with ammonia vapor; never pour ammonia on the leak. Use only the proper valve wrench, no longer than 6 inches, with no extensions or pipe wrenches. Vents sit low because chlorine is 2.5 times heavier than air, and fusible plugs melt at 158–165°F.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • Recommended residual near the end of the distribution system: about 0.5 mg/l free chlorine.
    • Chlorine gas is 2.5× heavier than air — vents and fans stay near the floor.
    • Fusible plugs melt at 158-165°F to prevent cylinder rupture.
    • 150-lb cylinder tare weight ≈ 92 lbs; a full ton container weighs ≈ 3,300 lbs.
    • Size a chlorinator to run at 1/4 to 1/3 of its rotometer scale under normal conditions.
    • Open a cylinder valve no more than one full turn; use a new lead gasket for every connection.
    • Chlorine IDLH 10 ppm; OSHA ceiling 1 ppm; flush eyes for 15 minutes.
    • EPA Risk Management Program applies above 2,500 lb of chlorine in a process.
