🔢Mathematics

The conversions, formulas, and worked examples every operator leans on — from unit conversions to area, volume, and dosage calculations.

On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    Don't panic — this chapter is mostly arithmetic wrapped in water-plant vocabulary. Memorize the area/volume formulas and the 8.34 conversion factor and you can solve most of the math questions on any class exam. When in doubt, convert everything to the same units before you calculate anything.

    1 Pure vs. concrete numbers, and units

    A pure number has no units (26, 195, ¾). A concrete number has specific units attached (26 psi, 195 gallons, 7.48 gallons per cubic foot). Always convert concrete numbers to the same units before calculating anything with them. Common water-industry units include pounds per day (ppd), gallons per minute (gpm), milligrams per liter (mg/l), and pounds per square inch (psi); larger Class A/B/C plants also use gallons per square foot, pounds per gallon, and million gallons per day (mgd).

    2 Fractions, decimals, and percentages

    To add or subtract fractions, first find a common denominator. To multiply fractions, multiply numerators together and denominators together. To divide, invert the divisor and multiply. For decimals: line up decimal points to add/subtract; multiply as whole numbers then count total decimal places to place the point; to divide, move the divisor's decimal point to the end, and move the dividend's decimal point the same number of places.

    To turn a decimal into a percent, move the decimal point two places right (0.50 → 50%). To turn a percent into a decimal, move it two places left. To find a percentage of a number, multiply by the decimal form (20% of 50 = 0.20 × 50 = 10).

    3 Exponents, ratio, proportion, and averages

    Exponentiation means multiplying a number by itself: 2² = 2×2 = 4, and 2³ = 2×2×2 = 8. A ratio (X:Y or X/Y) compares two numbers; a proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal, and you can cross-multiply to solve for an unknown — this is the basis of every dosage-rate calculation you'll do. An average is the sum of a data set divided by how many values are in it — used constantly for average daily demand, pump run times, and storage turnover.

    4 Area, perimeter, and volume

    Area is two dimensions multiplied together; volume is three. Always keep units consistent (convert inches to feet, etc., before multiplying). Key formulas:

    ShapeFormula
    Rectangle areaLength × Width
    Circle areaπ × D² ÷ 4 (π ≈ 3.14)
    Circle perimeterπ × D
    Rectangular tank volumeW × L × H
    Cylindrical tank volume3.14 × r² × H

    Example: a box 3 ft tall, 2 ft wide, 5 ft long has a volume of 3 × 2 × 5 = 30 cubic feet. A circular filter with a 20-foot diameter has an area of 3.14 × 10² = 314 square feet.

    5 The 8.34 conversion — dosage math

    One gallon of water weighs 8.34 pounds. That single number is the key to almost every chemical dosage problem you'll see: a dosage of 1 mg/l (= 1 ppm) in one million gallons of water equals 8.34 pounds of chemical. So for a chlorine feed of 5 ppm, you'd need 5 × 8.34 = 41.7 pounds of chlorine per million gallons treated. If the chemical isn't 100% pure — say 65% available chlorine like HTH — divide the pounds needed by the purity fraction to find how much actual product to feed.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • 8.34 lbs — weight of one gallon of water; the basis for every dosage calculation.
    • 1 mg/l = 1 ppm — and equals 8.34 lbs of chemical per million gallons of water.
    • π ≈ 3.14 — used in every circle/cylinder area and volume formula.
    • Circle area = π × D² ÷ 4; cylinder volume = 3.14 × r² × H.
    • 7.48 gallons per cubic foot — the conversion between cubic feet and gallons.
