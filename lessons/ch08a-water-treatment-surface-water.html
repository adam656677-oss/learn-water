Chapter 8 (Class A) · Mississippi Waterworks Operators Manual

🏞️Water Treatment — Surface Water

What's different about treating surface water — algae, pre-sedimentation, softening, and alternative disinfectants Class A operators must know.

Start reading Quiz this chapter
On this page

    Greg's take on this chapter

    Surface water brings challenges ground water mostly doesn't — algae, sudden quality swings, and disinfection byproducts. Focus on the softening methods (lime-soda ash vs. ion exchange) and know why THMs form and how plants avoid them — that's classic Class A exam territory.

    1 Surface water challenges

    Only about 10% of Mississippians get their water from surface sources (nationally it's 75%), but surface water is far more exposed to short-term weather and pollution than ground water. Algae, pathogenic bacteria, and other microscopic organisms are natural in surface sources and can foul pipelines, clog filters, cause corrosion, and create taste and odor problems. Screening (bar screens and wire mesh) removes large debris before it reaches plant equipment, and pre-sedimentation buffers sudden swings in raw-water quality — useful for river and stream sources.

    2 Coagulant aids and operational control

    A coagulant aid — activated silica, a weighting agent like bentonite clay, or a polyelectrolyte — builds stronger floc, overcomes temperature swings, or reduces the coagulant (and sludge) needed. Because surface water quality shifts with rain, runoff, and season, Class A plants constantly monitor turbidity, chlorine, temperature, pH, alkalinity, and color, using tools like the jar test and streaming current detectors to keep dosing on target. Short circuiting — water bypassing the normal route through a basin faster than the design detention time — is the main enemy of good sedimentation, usually caused by poor inlet baffling.

    3 Softening surface water

    Two methods dominate: lime-soda ash softening and ion-exchange softening. Lime-soda ash precipitates calcium carbonate (pH 9.4) and, with excess lime raising pH to 10.6, magnesium hydroxide; it's the practical choice for surface water since ion exchange isn't adaptable to it. Properly softened water usually sits around pH 11 and needs recarbonation (adding CO₂) afterward, ideally down to about pH 8.6, to stop calcium carbonate from depositing in the filters and distribution system. This method leaves 50-80 mg/l residual hardness — which actually helps protect against corrosion.

    4 Disinfection byproducts and alternative disinfectants

    Natural organic matter in surface water reacts with free chlorine residual to form trihalomethanes (THMs) — cancer-causing compounds, most commonly chloroform. Moving the chlorine injection point downstream, after coagulation/flocculation/sedimentation have removed the organic precursors, is one way to cut THM formation. Alternatives being used more:

    • Chloramines — less likely to form THMs, but roughly 200 times less effective at disinfection than free chlorine at typical surface-water pH, so dosage and contact time must be tightly controlled.
    • Chlorine dioxide — a strong oxidizer that doesn't form THMs, but does form chlorite/chlorate; EPA caps the combined residual of chlorine dioxide, chlorite, and chlorate at 1.0 mg/l. It's unstable and must be generated on-site.
    • Ozone — a powerful disinfectant that leaves no residual and doesn't form THMs, but is expensive to generate on-site.
    • Activated carbon (powdered or granular) — adsorbs organic THM precursors and tastes/odors rather than killing microbes directly.
    i
    Current rule check · Disinfection byproducts

    Under EPA's Stage 1 and Stage 2 Disinfectants and Disinfection Byproducts Rules, the MCLs are 0.080 mg/L for total trihalomethanes (TTHM) and 0.060 mg/L for the five haloacetic acids (HAA5), measured as locational running annual averages. Chlorine dioxide has an MRDL of 0.8 mg/L, and its byproduct chlorite has an MCL of 1.0 mg/L. The manual's combined 1.0 mg/l cap is older guidance.

    5 The Surface Water Treatment Rules in practice

    Every system using surface water, or ground water under the direct influence of surface water (GWUDI), must filter and disinfect to achieve at least 3-log (99.9%) removal or inactivation of Giardia and 4-log (99.99%) of viruses. Later rules added Cryptosporidium: at least 2-log removal, and more for sources that monitoring shows are at higher risk (LT2ESWTR).

    A well-run conventional plant earns much of that credit through filtration, and disinfection supplies the rest. Turbidity limits prove the filters are doing their part:

    • Combined filter effluent: 0.3 NTU or less in at least 95% of monthly readings, and never above 1 NTU.
    • Individual filters: turbidity monitored continuously and recorded every 15 minutes.
    • Disinfectant residual: at least 0.2 mg/L entering the distribution system (it can't stay below that for more than 4 hours), and a detectable residual throughout the system.

    6 CT: getting credit for disinfection

    CT = C × T: the disinfectant residual in mg/L times the contact time in minutes. The time used is T₁₀, the time it takes the first 10% of the water to pass through the basin, because water never flows perfectly evenly. Estimate it from the theoretical detention time and a baffling factor:

    BasinBaffling factor
    Unbaffled (mixed flow)0.1
    Poor0.3
    Average0.5
    Superior0.7
    Perfect (pipe flow)1.0

    Example. A 200,000-gallon clearwell at 1,400 gpm has a theoretical detention time of about 143 minutes. With poor baffling (0.3), T₁₀ = 43 minutes. At 1.0 mg/L free chlorine, CT = 1.0 × 43 = 43 mg·min/L. Compare that with EPA's CT tables for your water's temperature and pH. Cold water and high pH need much more CT, which is why winter is the hardest time to meet it.

    7 Taste, odor, and algae

    Earthy and musty tastes usually come from geosmin and MIB, made by blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) and certain soil bacteria. People notice them at just a few parts per trillion, so they cause complaints long before they pose any health risk. Common tools:

    • Powdered activated carbon, dosed from jar tests, adsorbs the compounds.
    • Oxidants such as permanganate or ozone destroy some odors.
    • Reservoir management, such as algaecides (only with state approval), aeration, and nutrient control, keeps blooms from forming.

    Some cyanobacteria also make toxins, such as microcystins. EPA's health advisories for microcystins are 0.3 µg/L for bottle-fed infants and preschool children and 1.6 µg/L for older children and adults. During a bloom, avoid heavy pre-oxidation that breaks algae cells open and releases toxin; remove the intact cells by coagulation and filtration instead.

    !
    Exam watch · THMs

    THMs form faster with more chlorine, more natural organic matter, higher temperature, higher pH, and longer contact time. Removing organics before chlorinating, by moving the chlorine point after sedimentation or using enhanced coagulation, is the classic fix. Chloramines form far fewer THMs but are much weaker disinfectants.

    📌 Numbers Greg wants you to memorize

    • Only about 10% of Mississippi's population is on surface water (vs. 75% nationally).
    • Lime-soda softening: calcium carbonate precipitates at pH 9.4; magnesium hydroxide needs pH 10.6.
    • Softened water is recarbonated back down to about pH 8.6.
    • Residual hardness after lime-soda softening: 50-80 mg/l — intentional, for corrosion protection.
    • Chlorine dioxide + chlorite + chlorate combined residual capped at 1.0 mg/l.
    • Surface Water Treatment Rules: 3-log Giardia, 4-log viruses, at least 2-log Cryptosporidium.
    • Combined filter effluent ≤ 0.3 NTU in 95% of readings, never over 1 NTU.
    • Residual entering the distribution system ≥ 0.2 mg/L (no more than 4 hours below).
    • CT = C × T₁₀; baffling factors run from 0.1 (unbaffled) to 1.0 (pipe flow).
