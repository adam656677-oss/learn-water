    Greg's take on water rules

    Rules make more sense once you see the pattern: Congress passes a law, EPA writes rules under it, and a state agency like MSDH or MDEQ enforces them. For the license exam, know the MCLs, the public notice tiers, and the coliform and lead rules. For the job, watch the dates on this page. 2027 is a big year: new Consumer Confidence Reports in January and the Lead and Copper Rule Improvements in November.

    1 How water law works

    A statute is a law passed by Congress, such as the Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) or the Clean Water Act (CWA). A statute tells EPA what to do. EPA then writes regulations, also called rules, that spell out the numbers, monitoring schedules, and reports. Rules are published in the Federal Register and collected in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR).

    How a drinking water rule is made

    1. Evidence. EPA studies health effects and how often a contaminant shows up, often using data from the Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (UCMR).
    2. Regulatory determination. EPA decides whether to regulate. The SDWA test has three parts: the contaminant may harm health, it occurs (or is likely to) at levels of concern, and regulating it offers a meaningful chance to reduce health risk.
    3. Proposed rule. Published in the Federal Register with a public comment period. The SDWA gives EPA 24 months after a decision to regulate to propose the rule, and 18 more months to finalize it.
    4. Final rule. Published with an effective date and, usually, a compliance date several years later.
    5. State adoption. States with primacy must adopt rules at least as strict as EPA's, generally within two years (extensions are possible).
    6. Court review. Anyone who objects to a national drinking water rule can ask the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit to review it.

    Where the rules live

    Primacy means a state runs the program itself. In Mississippi, the State Department of Health's Bureau of Public Water Supply has primacy for drinking water, and the Department of Environmental Quality (MDEQ) has run the Clean Water Act permit program (NPDES) since May 1, 1974.

    2 The Safe Drinking Water Act

    Signed on December 16, 1974, the SDWA covers every public water system in the country. It does not cover private household wells. It has been amended several times:

    • 1986: ordered EPA to regulate 83 named contaminants on a schedule, required filtration criteria for surface water and disinfection for all systems, and banned lead pipe, solder, and flux in new plumbing.
    • 1996: created the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund, Consumer Confidence Reports, operator certification guidelines, source water assessments, capacity development, the Contaminant Candidate List, and the UCMR program.
    • 2011: the Reduction of Lead in Drinking Water Act tightened the definition of “lead free” (effective January 4, 2014).
    • 2018: America's Water Infrastructure Act (AWIA) required risk and resilience assessments and emergency response plans, and expanded UCMR monitoring to systems serving 3,300 to 10,000 people.

    The standards vocabulary

    MCLG
    Maximum contaminant level goal: a non-enforceable health goal set where no harm is expected, with a margin of safety. It is zero for carcinogens and for disease-causing microbes.
    MCL
    Maximum contaminant level: the enforceable limit, set as close to the MCLG as feasible with the best available technology, taking cost into account.
    TT
    Treatment technique: a required process used instead of an MCL when a contaminant can't be measured practically, such as Cryptosporidium.
    Action level
    A trigger, not a limit. Exceeding the lead or copper action level requires the system to take specific steps.
    MRDL / MRDLG
    Maximum residual disinfectant level and its goal: limits for chlorine, chloramines, and chlorine dioxide.

    EPA also re-examines every existing standard in a six-year review, which is how rules like the microbial and disinfection byproduct rules end up being revised.

    !
    Exam watch · MCL vs. MCLG

    The MCL is the enforceable number. The MCLG is the health goal and is never enforceable. If a question asks what a system must meet, the answer is the MCL (or the treatment technique).

    3 Who the drinking water rules cover

    A public water system (PWS) provides water for human consumption through pipes or other constructed conveyances to at least 15 service connections, or regularly serves at least 25 people at least 60 days a year. There are three kinds:

    TypeDefinitionExamples
    Community (CWS)At least 15 connections used by year-round residents, or regularly serves at least 25 year-round residentsTowns, rural water associations, mobile home parks
    Non-transient non-community (NTNCWS)Regularly serves at least 25 of the same people at least 6 months a yearSchools, factories, and offices with their own water supply
    Transient non-community (TNCWS)Serves at least 25 people at least 60 days a year, but not the same peopleGas stations, campgrounds, rest areas, churches with their own well

    The type decides which rules apply. Community systems follow nearly all of them. Transient systems mostly worry about germs and nitrate, because their customers drink the water only briefly. A system that buys treated water from another system is a consecutive system. The seller, called the wholesale system, handles the treatment rules for its water, but the buyer is still responsible for its own distribution system.

    4 UCMR: how EPA finds the next contaminants

    The Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule comes from the 1996 SDWA amendments. Every five years EPA picks up to 30 contaminants that don't have drinking water standards yet and has water systems test for them. The results show how often, and at what levels, each one shows up nationwide. That data feeds the next regulatory determinations.

    • Who samples: every community and non-transient non-community system serving 3,300 or more people, plus a representative sample of smaller systems chosen by EPA. EPA pays the lab costs for the small systems it selects.
    • Where: mostly at entry points to the distribution system, after treatment.
    • How often: in recent cycles, surface water systems sampled four quarters in a row, and ground water systems sampled twice, five to seven months apart.
    • The data: EPA publishes it in its UCMR occurrence data and National Contaminant Occurrence Database. Community systems must also report detected UCMR results in their Consumer Confidence Report.
    CycleSamplingHighlights
    UCMR 12001–2005The first national screen of unregulated chemicals and microbes
    UCMR 22008–2010Pesticides, flame retardants, explosives, nitrosamines
    UCMR 32013–2015Six PFAS (including PFOA and PFOS), 1,4-dioxane, hexavalent chromium, chlorate, strontium, and two viruses at a small subset of systems
    UCMR 42018–2020Ten cyanotoxins, manganese and germanium, pesticides, brominated haloacetic acids, alcohols, and other organics
    UCMR 52023–202529 PFAS and lithium: the largest PFAS survey ever done
    UCMR 62028–2030 (proposed)Ultrashort PFAS such as TFA, pesticide metabolites, semivolatile and purgeable organics

    UCMR works with the Contaminant Candidate List (CCL), EPA's list of contaminants that may need regulation. The fifth list (CCL 5, final November 2022) named 66 chemicals, three chemical groups (PFAS, cyanotoxins, and disinfection byproducts), and 12 microbes.

    5 PFAS: where the drinking water rule stands

    PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are thousands of man-made chemicals with very strong carbon–fluorine bonds. They're used in nonstick, stain-resistant, and waterproof products and in some firefighting foams. They barely break down in the environment, which is why they're nicknamed “forever chemicals.”

    The April 2024 rule

    CompoundMCLGMCL
    PFOAZero4.0 ppt (ng/L)
    PFOSZero4.0 ppt
    PFHxS10 ppt10 ppt
    PFNA10 ppt10 ppt
    HFPO-DA (“GenX chemicals”)10 ppt10 ppt
    Mixtures of two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBSHazard Index of 1Hazard Index of 1

    Community and non-transient non-community systems had three years (to 2027) for initial monitoring and five years (to 2029) to meet the MCLs. Compliance is based on a running annual average.

    The Hazard Index adds up the ratio of each PFAS to its health-based water concentration. For example, PFHxS at 5 ppt is 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5 of the index. If the ratios for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS add up to more than 1, the mixture exceeds the standard.

    i
    Current rule check · PFAS, September 2026

    In May 2026 EPA proposed two changes: keep the 4.0 ppt PFOA and PFOS limits but move their compliance date to 2031, and rescind the limits for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and the Hazard Index mixture. The comment period closed July 20, 2026. These are proposals only; the court challenge to the 2024 rule is also still pending. Follow MSDH's direction on sampling in the meantime.

    How PFAS are removed

    Conventional treatment (coagulation, settling, filtration, chlorine) removes very little PFAS. Three technologies work: granular activated carbon (GAC), ion exchange resins made for PFAS, and reverse osmosis or nanofiltration membranes. Shorter-chain PFAS break through carbon sooner than PFOA and PFOS, and the ultrashort compounds on the UCMR 6 list are the hardest to remove. Spent carbon, resin, and membrane concentrate all need proper disposal.

    6 Lead and copper: three generations of rules

    Lead rarely comes from the source water. It gets into drinking water from lead service lines, lead solder, and brass fixtures, especially when the water is corrosive. So the rules focus on corrosion control, customer taps, and pipe replacement.

    RuleWhat it did
    Lead and Copper Rule (1991)Action levels at the 90th percentile of tap samples: lead 0.015 mg/L, copper 1.3 mg/L. Exceeding one triggers corrosion control treatment, source water treatment, public education, and lead service line replacement.
    Revisions (LCRR) (2021)Required a service line inventory by October 16, 2024, added a 0.010 mg/L “trigger level,” find-and-fix follow-up at high-lead homes, and testing in schools and child care facilities.
    Improvements (LCRI) (final October 2024, compliance November 1, 2027)Lowers the lead action level to 0.010 mg/L and drops the trigger level. Requires most systems to replace all lead and “galvanized requiring replacement” service lines within 10 years, at an average of about 10% a year. Homes with lead lines are sampled at the first and fifth liter, and the higher result counts. Systems with repeated exceedances must make filters available.

    To find the 90th percentile, sort the tap results from lowest to highest and multiply the number of samples by 0.9. With 20 samples, 20 × 0.9 = 18, so the 90th percentile is the 18th result in that list.

    !
    Exam watch · Action levels aren't MCLs

    Exceeding the lead action level is not an MCL violation. It triggers required actions. The MCLG for lead is zero; copper's MCLG is 1.3 mg/L.

    7 The Clean Water Act

    Enacted on October 18, 1972, over a presidential veto, the Clean Water Act's goal is to “restore and maintain the chemical, physical, and biological integrity of the Nation's waters.” The SDWA protects the water coming out of the tap. The CWA protects rivers, lakes, streams, and wetlands, which are also the source water for surface water plants.

    • NPDES permits (Section 402): discharging pollutants from a point source such as a pipe or ditch into waters of the United States requires a permit. Permits set effluent limits, monitoring, and reporting (discharge monitoring reports).
    • Secondary treatment (40 CFR 133): municipal sewage plants must meet 30-day averages of 30 mg/L BOD₅ and 30 mg/L TSS, 7-day averages of 45 mg/L, 85% removal, and pH 6.0–9.0.
    • Water quality standards (Section 303): each state assigns designated uses (such as drinking water supply, fishing, or swimming), sets criteria to protect them, and adopts an antidegradation policy.
    • Impaired waters and TMDLs (303(d)): waters that don't meet standards go on the 303(d) list, and states write a Total Maximum Daily Load: the most of a pollutant the water can take and still meet standards, divided among sources.
    • Dredge and fill (Section 404): filling wetlands or streams requires a permit from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers.
    • State certification (Section 401): states certify that federal permits will meet state water quality standards.
    • Pretreatment (Section 307, 40 CFR 403): industries that discharge to a city sewer must pretreat wastes that could pass through or upset the treatment plant.
    • Biosolids (Section 405, 40 CFR 503): limits for metals and pathogens, with Class A and Class B pathogen reduction, before sludge can be land-applied.
    • Stormwater: the 1987 Water Quality Act brought stormwater into NPDES. Construction sites disturbing one acre or more, industrial sites, and municipal separate storm sewer systems (MS4s) need permits.
    • Clean Water State Revolving Fund (1987) replaced construction grants with low-interest loans for sewer and water quality projects.

    How far the CWA reaches: WOTUS

    The CWA applies to “waters of the United States” (WOTUS). In Sackett v. EPA (May 25, 2023), the Supreme Court limited that to relatively permanent waters and wetlands with a continuous surface connection to them. EPA and the Army Corps issued a conforming rule in 2023, proposed a narrower definition in November 2025, and asked for more comment in September 2026.

    In Mississippi, MDEQ issues NPDES permits and the Mississippi Commission on Environmental Quality adopts the state's water quality standards. MDEQ also publishes the state's water quality assessments, including its ground water quality assessment.

    8 Other laws that reach the water plant

    Law or ruleWhat it means for a water utility
    EPCRA (community right-to-know)Chlorine is an extremely hazardous substance with a threshold planning quantity of 100 lb. Facilities storing that much or more must notify emergency planners and file a Tier II inventory report by March 1 each year.
    OSHA Process Safety Management (29 CFR 1910.119)Applies to processes with 1,500 lb or more of chlorine. It covers process hazard analysis, operating procedures, training, and management of change. Publicly owned utilities follow it where the state has an OSHA-approved plan covering public employers.
    EPA Risk Management Program (40 CFR 68)Applies to processes with more than 2,500 lb of chlorine (sulfur dioxide 5,000 lb; anhydrous ammonia 10,000 lb). Requires a hazard assessment, a prevention program, emergency response, and a risk management plan filed with EPA.
    OSHA confined spaces (29 CFR 1910.146)Permit-required confined space entry for tanks, vaults, and wet wells.
    SPCC (40 CFR 112)Oil spill plans where aboveground oil storage exceeds 1,320 gallons (counting containers of 55 gallons or more) or buried storage exceeds 42,000 gallons, and a discharge could reach navigable waters. Generator fuel tanks count.
    CERCLA (Superfund)PFOA and PFOS are hazardous substances (2024 designation, upheld in August 2026). This affects cleanup liability for contaminated sites.
    Underground Injection Control (SDWA Part C)Regulates six classes of injection wells to protect underground sources of drinking water.
    Sole source aquifers (SDWA §1424(e))EPA can designate an aquifer that supplies at least half of an area's drinking water. Federally funded projects in the area then get EPA review.
    “Lead free” (SDWA §1417)Since 2014, pipes, fittings, and fixtures may average no more than 0.25% lead on wetted surfaces, and solder and flux no more than 0.2%.
    AWIA Section 2013Community systems serving more than 3,300 people must complete a risk and resilience assessment, review it every five years, and update their emergency response plan within six months of each assessment. They certify both to EPA.

    Paying for it

    The Drinking Water SRF (1996) and Clean Water SRF (1987) lend money at low interest, and some goes out as principal forgiveness for disadvantaged communities. The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (November 15, 2021) added more than $50 billion for water, including $15 billion for lead service line replacement and $10 billion to address emerging contaminants such as PFAS. WIFIA (2014) makes long-term federal loans for large projects.

    Cybersecurity: there is no federal cybersecurity rule for water systems yet. EPA withdrew its 2023 memo that would have added cybersecurity to sanitary surveys after states challenged it in court. EPA still offers free assessments and guidance, and AWIA risk assessments must consider cyber threats.

    9 Mississippi: who does what

    AgencyRole
    MSDH Bureau of Public Water SupplyRuns the Safe Drinking Water Act program under state law (the Mississippi Safe Drinking Water Act of 1997, Miss. Code §41-26). It approves plans for new and modified systems, tracks monitoring and violations, inspects systems in sanitary surveys, issues boil water notices, certifies operators, and runs the state's cross-connection control and emergency-plan requirements.
    MDEQ (Department of Environmental Quality)Issues NPDES and stormwater permits, sets water quality standards through the Commission on Environmental Quality, issues surface water and ground water withdrawal permits, licenses water well contractors, runs dam safety, and studies the state's aquifers through its Office of Land and Water Resources.
    YMD (Yazoo Mississippi Delta Joint Water Management District)Formed in 1989 after the 1988 drought. It processes agricultural water use permits in the Delta, measures water levels in the alluvial aquifer, and promotes irrigation efficiency.
    EPA Region 4 (Atlanta)Oversees Mississippi's programs and can act directly, for example with emergency orders under SDWA Section 1431.

    State law declares that all water, “whether occurring on the surface of the ground or underneath the surface of the ground,” belongs to the people of Mississippi and is subject to regulation (Miss. Code §51-3-1). A ground water withdrawal permit from MDEQ is required for wells with a surface casing 6 inches or more in diameter. A well serving a single household for domestic use is exempt. Wells that are part of a public water system must also meet MSDH rules, whatever their size.

    Case study: Jackson's water system

    In late August 2022, problems at Jackson's O.B. Curtis treatment plant left much of the city without reliable water pressure. The U.S. Department of Justice brought a Safe Drinking Water Act case, and on November 29, 2022, a federal court appointed an interim third-party manager, Ted Henifin, to run the system. His company, JXN Water, operates it under the court's order, which was later expanded to the sewer system. As of 2026 the court is still overseeing the system, and questions about the transition to long-term local control are before the judge.
